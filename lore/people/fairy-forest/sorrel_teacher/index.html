    Sorrel¶

    Teacher of Listening
    Town Fairy Forest
    Where Grove School
    Role Scholar
    Age Adult
    In the game Sorrel

    Personality¶

    Soft-spoken and unhurried, he believes most mistakes come from talking too much. His lessons are mostly silence, and his students love him anyway.

    Lore¶

    Sorrel teaches Listening at the Grove School: the first and oldest Magician lesson, in which a student learns to hear the trees, the wind and the creatures of the forest before trying any magic at all. He is forty-five, soft-spoken and unhurried, and he believes most mistakes come from talking too much. His lessons are mostly silence. His students complain about this loudly and constantly, and they love him anyway, which he finds mildly puzzling and does not try to explain.

    Calyra's pupil¶

    Sorrel grew up in the forest and came to the Grove School at eight, a small, quiet boy who was already better at hearing things than at saying them. His first teacher was Calyra, who taught listening to the youngest students for forty years before the forest chose her as Eldest. She was the first grown-up he had met who did not mind a silence, and he followed her lessons for years after he was meant to have finished them.

    At twenty-eight he came back to the school to help her, sitting at the side of her lessons, walking the youngest students out to their trees and fetching the ones who wandered off. When Calyra became Eldest nine years ago, the lesson passed to him. He still teaches it almost exactly as she did, except that he says even less. Calyra, who visits the school more often than an Eldest strictly needs to, once sat at the back of his class for a whole morning and afterwards told him only, "Good." He has kept that word ever since.

    He never married. He lives alone in a small tree home a short walk from the school, and he spends his evenings walking the paths with his hands in his pockets, listening.

    The lessons¶

    A listening lesson with Sorrel begins with a walk and ends with a question. In between, the students sit with their backs to a tree and are quiet for as long as they can bear. Then he asks what they heard, and he accepts no answer that sounds like something they were told. The best students take a year to understand what he wants. Some never do, and he teaches them just as patiently.

    Liri, Tansy's daughter, is in his youngest class this year. She is bright, bold and wants the exciting spells first and the listening never, and she can now sit still in his lessons for almost a quarter of an hour, which Sorrel calls progress and Liri calls torture. He likes her very much. He thinks the children who fight the silence hardest often hear the most once they stop.

    Aurel, the junior teacher, is his student in a different sense. Aurel finished his own schooling three years ago and now teaches the youngest, and he writes every lesson out word for word and then forgets the notes. Sorrel thinks this is the best thing that could happen to him and has never once suggested he stop writing them. When Aurel comes to him in a panic after a lesson has gone wrong, Sorrel listens to the whole story, says one sentence, and sends him home to sleep.

    Amaryl, who teaches Growing, has worked beside him for many years. She thinks his lessons are too quiet; he thinks hers are too loud. Each of them sends their best students to the other.

    The uneasy edges¶

    Lately Sorrel has been saying that the forest sounds uneasy at its edges, and he cannot explain what he means. It is not a noise, exactly. On his dawn walks to the outer lanterns the birds seem to start their singing a little later than they used to, and stop a little sooner. The wind in the outer trees has a note in it he does not recognise. Twice he has stood at the last lantern and had the feeling, which he would never put in a lesson, that the woods beyond were listening back.

    The edge watch knows him by sight now, the quiet teacher who stands at the outer lanterns at dawn with his eyes closed. He has tried to describe what he hears to the Council and given up halfway through a sentence. He is not the sort of man who likes to frighten people, and he does not know whether he should be frightened himself.

    What he wants and fears¶

    Sorrel wants every child who passes through the Grove School to leave knowing how to be quiet long enough to hear something. He fears that what he is hearing at the edges is real, that it is growing, and that the forest's oldest lesson may turn out to be the only warning it gets.

    Sorrel also teaches Blink, the short step forward through the air that the Grove School teaches anyone with patience, magician or not, to any traveller who can sit through one of his listening lessons without fidgeting.

    A quest might ask a traveller to walk with Sorrel to the outer lanterns at dawn and tell him honestly what they hear, to help Aurel through a lesson Sorrel has quietly arranged to miss, or to follow a sound in the outer woods that Sorrel cannot name.
