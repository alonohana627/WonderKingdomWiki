You can hear Hob before you see him. He whistles along with the creak of the windmill sails, a tune that changes with the wind, and when the wind drops the whistling stops and he goes out to look at the sky. He is forty-four, easygoing, broad in the shoulder and dusty from head to toe; the flour has settled so deep into his eyebrows that his daughter says he was born with white ones.

Hob runs the windmills in Kaldemor Park and grinds the flour for half the town. Rosa's terrace oven comes first, always, and every morning before the sun is properly up the first sacks of the day go down to the Market for her. Then come the farm kitchens, the Sleepy Snail, and the households that still bake their own. In autumn he grinds the harvest grain for the carts that go down to Bain Harbour, and the mill runs from dark to dark.

### The woodcutter's son

He is the son of Orin and Wren. Orin felled trees for forty winters, and in Kaldemor the sons and daughters of woodcutters have always taken up the axe after their parents. Hob is the only one who never wanted to. As a boy he was handed a hatchet and spent the afternoon not chopping wood but watching the windmill sails turn on the far side of the Park, and he has been watching them ever since. At fourteen he began carrying sacks for the old miller, Tibbet, and by twenty he was greasing the gears and dressing the millstones himself. When Tibbet died, Hob was the only person in town who knew how the mills were put together, and the mills became his.

Orin was disappointed for about a week and has been proud ever since. He tells everyone who will listen, which is everyone in the Park, that his son "fells wheat instead of trees". Hob has heard the joke perhaps a thousand times and still laughs, because his father enjoys it so. Wren never minded which he chose, so long as he came home for supper.

### Mabel and Nell

Hob married Mabel the potter twelve years ago. They met over a broken flour crock: he brought it to her to mend, she told him it was past mending and threw him a new one while he waited, and he came back the next week with another crock he had broken on purpose. She knew, and said nothing, and threw him that one too. Their house stands near the windmills, and its shelves are full of crocks.

Their daughter Nell is eight and spends her afternoons flat on her back in the square counting clouds. Hob understands her better than most people do. A miller spends his life looking up, judging the wind by the clouds, and he was the one who stitched her little tally book from the brown paper his flour sacks come wrapped in. He has never asked her how big the number is. He suspects it is very large and does not want to spoil it.

### Weather

Hob worries about the weather the way other people worry about money. Too little wind and the stones stand still and the town goes short of bread; too much and he has to reef the sails in a hurry before they tear. A wet summer spoils the grain. A dry one stunts it. He knows the sky over Kaldemor better than anyone except his daughter, and he does not like what he has seen of it lately: the winds coming in from the woods past the hills have changed, and twice this spring, on mornings with no wind at all, his sails shuddered and the stones rattled in their beds, as if the ground had shrugged beneath the mills.

He has not told anyone about that, not even Mabel. He has decided it was nothing. He checks the foundations every morning anyway.

### What he wants

Hob wants plenty of grain, a steady westerly, and his father to stay well enough to sit on his bench in the afternoons. He would like Nell to be a miller one day, and knows she will probably be something no one has thought of yet. He is quietly glad that Fen carries his flour to Rosa twice a week, because it means someone is looking after Rosa, who he thinks has been lonely for years.

### Hooks

A quest might begin when a millstone cracks before the harvest, and the only stone fit to replace it lies in an old quarry past the hills where the snails are thick. Or a traveller might help Hob find out why his sails shudder on still mornings, and whether something is moving beneath the ground when they do.
