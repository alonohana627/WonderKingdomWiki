Lottie's spectacles are on her head. They are nearly always on her head, and she is nearly always looking for them, and the children in her schoolroom have long since stopped telling her, because watching her find them is the best part of the morning. She is thirty-four, kind, curious and a little scatterbrained, with chalk on her sleeves and ink on her thumb, and she teaches every child in Kaldemor who is old enough to sit still and several who are not. She has one rule for herself, and she has never broken it: she never answers a child's question with "because".

### The schoolroom in the tree

Lottie's schoolroom is at the very top of the great treehouse, behind the town square, in a round room whose windows look out over the roofs of the mushroom houses to the windmills and the hills. The floor creaks, the walls lean a little where the living tree has pushed them, and on windy days the whole room sways very gently, which the children love and Lottie pretends not to notice. Alfie and his carpenters keep it in repair. Lottie keeps it in chalk, slates and pressed leaves, and in a great many questions.

She was born in Kaldemor, the daughter of a cart-painter and a beekeeper, both dead now, and she was a pupil in the same room herself. Her own teacher was old Philippa, who taught Kaldemor's children for more than forty years and who once answered the small Lottie's question about the stars by taking the whole class up onto the treehouse roof at night. Lottie decided that evening what she wanted to be. She went to Selsior at eighteen to study in the Scholars' Quarter, came home at twenty-five when Philippa grew too frail to climb the stairs, and has taught in the treehouse for nine years. Philippa died the following winter, and Lottie still keeps her ruler on the windowsill, though she has never once used it on anything but a straight line.

### Nell, Fen and the rest

Her pupils are as different as the mushroom houses. Nell, the miller's girl, is very good at sums and very bad at looking at the board, because she is usually looking at the sky through the window; Lottie once spent a whole lesson explaining why clouds float, and Nell wrote down every word and then asked why they bother. Lottie did not have an answer, and said so, and went to look one up. Little Fen asks "but why?" until even Lottie has to go and look something up, and Lottie thinks this is the finest thing a pupil can do. It was Lottie who first told Fen the story of the one seed and the generous soil, and she has since found small holes in odd corners of the schoolroom's window boxes, where Fen has been checking.

### The one-seed story

Lottie collects every version of the one-seed story she can find. Everyone in Kaldemor knows the plain one: a traveller crossing the eastern meadows planted a single seed, slept beside it, and woke to find a tree taller than a tower. But the old people tell it differently. In one version the traveller sang to the seed. In another the traveller wept on it. In one the tree was already there, small and hidden, and the traveller only noticed it. Lottie writes each down in a fat green notebook, with who told it and when, and she has nearly two hundred pages. She has asked Orin, Wren and Ned, and Ned told her a version so short and grumpy that she wrote it down twice to be sure. Some say the seed came from the World Tree. Lottie would dearly like to know, and has written that question on the inside cover, where she will see it every time she opens the book.

### Hard questions

Lately the children ask her harder things. They ask why there are more snails in the meadow, and what is in the hollow past the woods, and why Rufus reads the road warnings in a softer voice now. Tam asked her last week whether the old seal under the World Tree was going to break. Lottie told them what the history books say: that the four peoples sealed the Balrog away long ago, that the seal was damaged fifty years ago, and that the guardians below still keep watch. Then a small voice asked what would happen if it broke, and for the first time in nine years Lottie did not know what to say. She did not say "because". She said, "Nobody knows yet, and I would rather tell you that than make something up." Then she went home and did not sleep.

### What she wants and fears

Lottie wants her children to grow up curious rather than frightened, and she wants a true ending to the one-seed story. She fears that one day a question will come that she ought to answer and cannot, and that a child will learn from her face what she would not say aloud.

### Hooks

One version in Lottie's green notebook, told to her long ago by an old woman of the farms, says that the traveller left a sign on the tree before walking on, "so it would know her when she came back". Lottie has never found the sign and would like a traveller's help looking. She would also be very grateful for anyone who could bring her a version of the story from another town.
