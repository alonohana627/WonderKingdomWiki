Mabel throws the pots of Kaldemor. If you have eaten soup in this town, it was out of one of her bowls; if you have bought pickles from Old Marta, they came out of one of her crocks; and if you have drunk cider at the Sleepy Snail, the jug was hers too. She is thirty-nine, calm, practical and good with her hands, with clay drying grey to the elbow most of the day, and she can tell what a pot is for before it leaves the wheel. When something amuses her she laughs from the belly, loud enough to be heard three workshops down, and the whole Workshops lane has learned to smile when it hears her.

### Learning the wheel

Mabel was born in Kaldemor, the daughter of a thatcher and his wife, both long dead now. She was never any good on a roof. At fifteen she went to sit in the doorway of the pottery on the Workshops lane and watch Primrose, the mayor's wife, throw a jug, and she sat there every day for a week until Primrose put a lump of clay in her hands to make her stop staring. Mabel worked under Primrose for twelve years. Primrose taught her to centre the clay, to pull a wall thin and even, to judge a kiln by its colour, and to talk to customers as if they were neighbours, which in Kaldemor they always are.

When Primrose fell ill in her last year, Mabel ran the wheel for her. Primrose died twelve years ago, and the workshop passed to Mabel. Mayor Tobin still comes by now and then to sit on the stool by the door, as he used to when his wife worked there, and tells Mabel she pulls a handle exactly as Primrose did. Mabel keeps one of Primrose's jugs on the top shelf. It is not for sale, and she has never put anything in it.

### Hob

It was in that last year, with Primrose ill, that a dusty young miller came in with a broken flour crock and asked whether it could be mended. Mabel told him it was past mending and threw him a new one while he waited. The next week he came back with another broken crock. She knew perfectly well he had broken it on purpose, and she said nothing, and threw him that one too. They were married twelve years ago, a few months after Primrose died, and Mabel moved into Hob's house near the windmills. The shelves there are now full of crocks, which she says is his own fault.

Hob's mother Wren tells Mabel she is the best thing that ever happened to him. It embarrasses them both, and Mabel secretly agrees.

### Nell

Their daughter Nell is eight and spends her afternoons flat on her back in the town square, counting clouds. Mabel says that Nell learned to look up before she learned to walk, and that it was the windmill sails over her cot that did it. For years Mabel stood in the doorway at dusk calling her in. Nell never came. Mabel long ago gave up, and now simply sends supper out to the square in a covered bowl when the light goes, and the bowl comes back empty and the girl comes back later. She has never asked how big the number in the tally book is. She thinks a child should be allowed one secret that is entirely her own.

### The kiln and the carts

Mabel works hard all year and hardest in late summer, when she fills the crates of pottery that go down to Bain Harbour on the harvest carts. Last autumn one of those carts never arrived, and with it went a whole season's worth of her best blue-glazed bowls. She was cross about the bowls for a day and frightened about the drivers for a good deal longer. They were not hers, but she had packed their straw.

### What she wants and fears

Mabel wants a good kiln, a dry autumn and her family round her table, which is harder than it sounds with a husband who goes out to look at the sky and a daughter who will not come in from it. She has noticed that Hob has started checking the mill foundations every morning, and that he says it is nothing. She has decided to believe him until he tells her otherwise. What she fears is the thing she cannot shape: the roads, the woods and whatever is making the town talk in low voices.

### Hooks

On one still, windless morning this summer, a row of Mabel's new jugs walked themselves to the edge of the drying shelf, and one fell. She blamed the mill. A traveller who mentions it to Hob may find out that she is not the only one who has noticed. Mayor Tobin, too, has asked for great crocks to keep the ground snail shell dry for his garden fence, bigger than any Mabel has thrown, and she will need help digging the clay for them from the bank past the farms, where the snails are thick.
