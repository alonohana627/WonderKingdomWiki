Dottie is ten years old and she is not too young. She would like that understood before anything else. She is small, sunburnt and very determined, with a crook taller than she is and a whistle on a string round her neck, and she minds her grandmother Elsie's flock of sheep on the hills above the farms all by herself. She counts them in the morning, counts them at noon, and counts them twice before bed, once for herself and once in case the first count was wrong.

### Grandmother Elsie

Dottie lives with her grandmother Elsie, the dairy farmer, in the stone farmhouse with the cheese room at the back. Her parents, Elsie's daughter Hyacinth and Hyacinth's husband Tom, died of a winter fever eight years ago, when Dottie was two, and she does not remember them, though she has her mother's crook and her father's whistle. Elsie raised her. Elsie is proud, plain and up before the sun, and has a firm opinion about everything and no patience for fuss, and Dottie is so like her that the farm hands say it is like watching the same woman at both ends of her life.

Elsie gave Dottie the sheep when she was seven, the year Elsie's knees decided they were finished with hills. The cows and the cheese are Elsie's. The sheep are Dottie's. Dottie takes this very seriously.

### The flock

There are thirty-one sheep and, until a week ago, nine lambs. Dottie knows every one of them by face and most of them by voice. The big ewe with the black ear is Duchess. The ram is Mister Pike, who is not to be turned one's back on. The smallest lamb of the spring, a speckled one with a bent ear, is Button. She shears them each summer with Elsie watching from the gate, and sells the wool to Winnie the weaver in the Workshops, who spins it into yarn, some of which goes to old Wren in the Park to become caps for every child in Kaldemor, Dottie included. Winnie pays her fairly and never once has said she is too young, which is why Winnie is Dottie's favourite grown-up after her grandmother.

### Button

A week ago, on a misty morning, Button wandered off toward the woods while Dottie was counting the others, and has not come back.

Dottie went looking as far as she dared, which was further than she should have, and found nothing but a tuft of speckled wool on a bramble at the edge of the trees. She has gone back every evening since, and every evening she has turned round at the same bramble, because the woods are darker than they used to be and the grown-ups talk about Shrooms and something big in a hollow.

She has not told her grandmother. She means to. But Elsie gave her the sheep because she trusted her, and Dottie cannot bear to see her face when she hears that a lamb is lost. So she has said nothing, and counted forty every night out loud for Elsie, and thirty-nine under her breath. She has been so busy searching that she has brought Winnie less wool than usual, and Winnie has noticed, and so has Wren. Dottie knows they have noticed. She has decided that if she does not look at them, it does not count.

### What she wants and fears

Dottie wants Button home and nobody ever to know he was gone. She wants to be allowed to drive the flock to the far hill pasture alone, and to have a dog, and to be taken seriously by Quill, though she does not want to be a warrior, only to be asked. What she fears is that Button is gone for good, and that it is her fault, and that her grandmother will say, kindly, that she is too young after all.

### Hooks

A traveller who finds Dottie at the bramble at dusk might be asked, very fiercely, to come with her into the woods to look for Button, and to promise not to tell. The tuft of wool on the bramble is not the only thing she found there: there were tracks beside it, large and round and damp, and she has not told anyone about those either.
