If you cross the town square of Kaldemor on a fine afternoon and nearly tread on a small girl lying flat on her back in a knitted cap, that is Nell, and you have just interrupted her. She will tell you so. "Shh," she says, without looking away from the sky, "I am counting the clouds." Then she will go on counting, and you will be forgiven only if you stand very still until she has finished.

Nell is eight years old. She is the only child of Hob the miller and Mabel the potter, and the granddaughter of Orin and Wren, who spend their afternoons on a bench in the Park. She was born in the family's house near the windmills, and some of her first memories are of the sails turning against the sky above her cot. Her mother says that is where the trouble started: Nell learned to look up before she learned to walk.

### The tally book

Nell does not simply watch clouds. She counts them, and she is very serious about it. She keeps a little book with a stitched spine, which her father made for her from the brown paper the flour sacks come wrapped in, and in it she keeps a tally of every cloud she has ever counted. Each day gets a line. Each line gets its marks, and beside the marks she writes down what the best clouds looked like: a snail, a cart, a teapot, a snail again, a cat asleep on a fence, Grandpa's nose. Snails come up more often than anything else, which she says is because Kaldemor has so many of them that even the sky has caught the habit.

She will not tell anyone how big the total is. Not her mother, not her teacher Lottie, not Mayor Tobin, who has tried to guess it at least once a month for a year. She says that if you say a number like that out loud it stops being yours. Her grandfather Orin, who counts trees from his bench the way she counts clouds, is the only person she has ever shown the last page, and he swears he could not read it without his glasses, which is probably true.

Her grandmother Wren knitted the cap she wears, a soft blue thing with a lopsided bobble, and Nell has not willingly taken it off since. Wren knits caps for every child in Kaldemor; Nell is the one who wears hers to bed. Her mother long ago gave up calling her in from the clouds and now simply sends supper out to the square when the light goes.

### Friends and lessons

Nell's two best friends could not be less like her. Tam is loud and swings a stick at anything that looks like an enemy; Fen notices every small thing and asks a question about each of them. The three of them can usually be found together, Tam shouting, Fen asking and Nell shushing them both. Tam is not allowed to swing his stick within reach of the tally book. Fen is allowed to help count, but only the small clouds.

At Lottie's school in the great treehouse, Nell is very good at sums and very bad at looking at the board. Lottie, who never answers a question with "because", once spent a whole lesson explaining why clouds float, and Nell wrote down every word and then asked why they bother.

### What she wants and fears

Nell wants to count every cloud there is, which she admits might take a while. Lately she has a second ambition: to find out where the clouds go after they pass the hills, and whether they are counted again somewhere else. She is a little afraid that someone in another town is counting the same clouds, and that it would not be fair.

What she really fears she does not write in the book. Since the spring, she has seen her grandfather fall asleep on his bench more often, and she counts a little faster in the afternoons, as if she could get more done while he is still awake to hear about it.

### Hooks

On three evenings this summer Nell has written something odd in her book: a low grey cloud above the woods past the hills that did not move with the others, and that was gone by morning. She drew it as a snail, like all the rest, and has not shown the page to anyone. A traveller who earns her trust might be the first to see it. Another quest might begin the day the tally book goes missing from the square, and Nell, for once, cannot stay quiet about it.
