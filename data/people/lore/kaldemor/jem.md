You will hear Jem before you see him, and if you stop to talk to him, he will be humming before you have finished your first sentence. By the end of your second, he will have made up a song about you. It will rhyme your name with something unfortunate, and he will play it on his fiddle in the square that afternoon, and by evening half of Kaldemor will be whistling it. He is twenty-four, cheerful and restless, all elbows and knees and a foot that never stops tapping, with a battered brown fiddle that he carries everywhere, even to bed, his landlady says.

### The fiddle

Jem was born in Kaldemor, in a narrow house behind the Market. The fiddle belonged to his grandmother, Nan Dilly, who played every harvest dance in Kaldemor for forty years and who taught him to hold the bow before he could hold a spoon. When she died, nine years ago, Jem was fifteen, and that autumn he stood up on the cart at the harvest fair and played the dances himself, badly, and then a little better each year. His parents moved to Selsior when he was eighteen to keep a lodging house, and wanted him to come; he would not leave the fiddle's town, and they live there still and write when they remember to. No harvest dance in Kaldemor has started without him since.

By day he plays in the town square, for the children and the newcomers and anyone waiting for Pell's cab. By night he plays at the Sleepy Snail, where Molly feeds him in exchange, rather more than he can eat, and scolds him for not finishing it. Molly is the nearest thing he has to family in Kaldemor now, and she knows it, and fusses accordingly. He lodges in the room over her kitchen. Fewer travellers come in from the east these days, and the ones who do arrive tired and frightened, and Jem plays them something soft and does not make up songs about them.

### The song

For a year now, Jem has been writing a song for Daisy the painter.

He has written a great many songs, about everyone in Kaldemor, and every one of them took him an afternoon. This one has taken a year and is not finished. He has written verses about her paint-spotted nose and her colours and her laugh, and each time he gets to the end, he decides it is not good enough, and starts again. He has played pieces of it in the Workshops lane, where she works, and stopped halfway and played something else. He has written forty-three verses. He has crossed out forty-one of them. He made up a song about Lia and Clem in an afternoon, and Clem begged him not to play it, and Jem thought that was very unfair, since it was a good song.

Daisy has no idea. Or she pretends not to. Jem does not know which, and is too frightened to find out. Her grandfather Bertie knows exactly what is going on and winks at Jem in a way that makes him miss notes.

### What he wants and fears

Jem wants to finish the song, play it to Daisy, and live through it. He would like, too, one day, to play in the streets of Selsior, where his grandmother always said the crowds danced longest on Veligor. He fears that the song will never be good enough. He fears, more quietly, that the roads are becoming too dangerous for fiddlers, and that one autumn the harvest carts will not go down to Bain Harbour at all, and there will be no fair to play, and the old tunes his grandmother taught him will have no one to dance to them.

### Hooks

Jem has heard there is an old dancing tune his grandmother used to play, one he never learned, known by heart only to the oldest people in town. A traveller might be asked to coax it out of Orin, or Ned, or Wren, a verse at a time. Or he might at last decide the song for Daisy is finished, and need someone to make sure she is in the square, without paint on her nose, at exactly the right moment.
