Barnaby is the bosun of the Bright Morrow, and he is the biggest man on the Bain Harbour docks and very nearly the loudest. He is forty-six, big, booming and soft-hearted, with a laugh that rattles the rigging and a story for every scar. He has a great many scars. No one is sure how many of the stories are true, including, on some evenings, Barnaby.

### A story for every scar

Barnaby has been at sea since he was a boy, and he has been bosun of the Bright Morrow for nine years, since the day Captain Seraphine bought her. As bosun he looks after the ship's rigging, sails, anchors and boats, and the crew who work them, and he does it with enormous care and a great deal of noise. He can splice a line faster than anyone in the harbour and sings while he does it, badly. The crew love him. They also know that if they leave a rope uncoiled on his deck, the whole Waterfront will hear about it.

The scar across his left hand, he says, came from a swordfish that leapt over the bow and tried to steal his dinner. The one on his chin came from a fight with three smugglers in a sea cave, or from a gull, depending on who is listening. The long one on his forearm is from a parted line in a storm, and that one is true, because Seraphine was there; she says he caught the line before it took a sailor's head off, and Barnaby, for once, does not tell that story at all.

His most devoted listener is Finch, the dock errand runner, who sits on the bollards beside the Bright Morrow whenever she is in harbour and hears every story twice. Barnaby gives him the best ones. He has never had a son, and he would not say that is what Finch is to him, but he keeps a twist of barley sugar in his coat pocket for the boy on every voyage, and it has never once come home uneaten.

### The eyes

Last autumn, on a moonlit night far out in deep water, Barnaby was alone on deck with the watch asleep at the wheel, and he looked over the side. A pair of eyes as large as cartwheels looked back up at him from under the Bright Morrow's hull. They did not blink. Then they sank away into the dark, slowly, as if whatever owned them was in no hurry at all.

Barnaby told the story on the Waterfront the next day, and the Waterfront laughed, because it was Barnaby, and because the eyes were as large as cartwheels. He laughed along. He has told it many times since, and it is the only one of his stories that has never grown in the telling. Two people have never laughed at it. One is Finch, who believes every word. The other is Captain Seraphine, who listened to it once in silence and then went to her cabin and shut the door. Barnaby has thought about that a great deal.

He has not slept on deck since. He used to sling a hammock under the stars on every warm night of every voyage. Now he sleeps below, in the forecastle with the crew, and when the others tease him about it he tells them his back is getting old. It is not his back.

### What he wants and fears

Barnaby wants someone to believe him who is not ten years old. He fears that if he ever looks over the side again on a moonlit night, the eyes will be closer.

### Hooks

A quest could stand the night watch on the Bright Morrow and look over the side for him, sort the true stories from the tall ones, or find out what Seraphine knows that made her shut her cabin door.
