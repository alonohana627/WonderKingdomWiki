Wenna mends nets on the fish market steps, as she has done all her life, and from her step she sees everyone who comes into the Fish Market and knows the business of most of them. She is seventy-one, sharp-tongued, proud and quick with her fingers, and she approves of very little of what she sees. Her netting needle moves so fast it blurs. Her opinions move faster.

### The steps

Wenna's mother mended nets on the same steps before her, and Wenna sat beside her from the age of five, filling needles with twine and learning every knot a fishing net can hold. By the time she was twelve she could mend a hole faster than her mother, and she has never let anyone forget it. Every fishing family in the bay brings her their nets, and she can tell, by looking at a tear, what made it: rocks fray, dogfish chew, seals bite round holes, and careless fishermen leave knots she describes to their faces at length.

She married Davey, a fisherman of the bay, who fished from the Puffin, the little open boat his own father had built. Davey was the quietest man in the harbour and the only one who could make Wenna laugh without trying. He died of a winter fever twelve years ago. She mended his last net the week after and gave it to their son.

That son is Cobb, who fishes from the Puffin now and is as quiet as his father was. Wenna thinks this is a great mercy, since the rest of the family is not. Cobb married Gwennol, the fishmonger, whose voice carries across the whole market, and Wenna has argued with her daughter-in-law about the price of fish, the gutting of fish and the raising of children for twenty years. Gwennol is the only person in the Fish Market who argues back properly, and Wenna respects her more than she will ever admit.

### The grandchildren

Her grandson Lowen has just signed on as a deckhand aboard Captain Corwin's Heron, against his father's wishes. Wenna told the whole market that the boy was a fool, and then sewed him a new canvas kit bag with a double seam, because a fool should at least have a bag that does not split. Her granddaughter Bree is bossy, fearless and runs a business in crab shells from the rock pools; Wenna says she gets it from her mother and is privately sure she gets it from her grandmother.

### The day Kenver died

Wenna was mending beside Kenver, the lighthouse keeper's husband, sixteen years ago on the morning he died. One moment he was singing over his nets, the next he was gone. She sent a boy running for Clemence, the healer, and held Kenver's hand until she came, though there was nothing anyone could do. She has never said much about that morning. She still keeps his place on the step clear, and she still finishes mending on the side of the steps where he sat.

### The torn nets

Twice this year Cobb's nets have come up torn clean through, both times in the deeper water just inside the headland. Wenna mended both. They were not frayed or chewed or bitten. They were opened in long straight rents, longer than her arm, as clean as if a blade had been drawn through them, and yet the edges were not cut but pulled. She announced to the whole market that no fish she ever knew made tears like that, and she has repeated it to everyone since. She has also kept a strip of the torn net, which she has shown nobody, rolled up in her basket under the spare twine.

What frightens her is not the tears. It is that Cobb has stopped going out past the middle of the bay, and that her son, who has never been afraid of the water in his life, has not said why.

### The steps' company

Barnacle, the harbour cat, sleeps in the sun on the steps beside her and accepts fish heads from Wenna with great dignity. Wenna says Barnacle is the only creature in the market with any sense. Lately Nerissa, the pearl diver, has taken to sitting on the step below hers on days she would once have spent diving. Wenna has asked her nothing, which for Wenna is a kindness of enormous size.

### Hooks

A quest could take Wenna's strip of torn net to someone who might know what made it, go out with Cobb to the place where the nets were torn, or win Wenna's approval, which nobody in living memory has managed on the first try.
