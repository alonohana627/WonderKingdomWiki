Hobart keeps the harbour warehouses of Bain Harbour, the long stone sheds behind the docks where every cargo goes between ship and buyer. He is seventy-eight, methodical and dry, and slightly deaf in his left ear, which he uses to his advantage: he hears what he wants to hear and nothing else, and he always seems to hear the other side of an argument with that ear. He trusts his own counting over anyone's ledger, including his own.

### Fifty years of crates

Hobart came to the warehouses at twenty as a porter, when they were kept by an old warehouseman whose name he will tell you only if you have a long afternoon. He was slow and he was careful, and the old man noticed that the slow, careful porter never once lost a crate. At twenty-eight Hobart took the keys, and he has kept them for fifty years, longer than Garrow, the dock foreman, has been alive.

He lost the hearing in his left ear in his thirties, when a block came loose in a gale and swung past his head close enough to ring it like a bell. He says the ear has been very useful ever since. He never married. He lives in two rooms above the oldest of the warehouses, with a window that looks out over the roofs to the bay, and he says he sleeps better with the smell of tar and rope below him than he would anywhere else.

### The count

Hobart's rule is simple: never trust a manifest until you have counted it yourself. Captains write what they loaded, merchants write what they paid for, and clerks write what they were told, and none of it is the same as what is actually in the shed. So Hobart counts every cargo that comes in, crate by crate, sack by sack, cask by cask, with a stub of chalk and a tally on the door post, and only then does he write it in his book. Ottilie, the clerk of the Harbour Hall, has been known to come down to the warehouses with her ledger to compare numbers. She is the only person whose figures he respects, and he has never told her so.

He taught Garrow to count like that when Garrow was a boy porter of fourteen, thirty-five years ago, and they have argued about the counts every week since. Garrow comes by every evening on his way home, shouting before he is through the door, and Hobart lets him shout and then tells him the right number. Garrow pretends to find him maddening. Hobart pretends not to have heard. They are each other's oldest friend, and neither has ever said that aloud either.

He has strong views on cargo. He likes salt, which stacks; he dislikes fish, which does not keep; and he complains every time he has to count Marisol's lead-lined tea chests, which weigh twice what they ought to. At night he leaves a side door open a crack for Barnacle, the harbour cat, who hunts the rats among the sacks. He says she is the only member of staff who never miscounts.

### The oldest crates

At the very back of the oldest warehouse, behind three generations of forgotten barrels, stand four crates of dark, heavy wood, bound with iron gone almost black, stamped with a mark nobody in the harbour recognises. Hobart claims they have been there since Old Bain's day. They appear on the first page of the oldest ledger in his office, in a hand so faded nobody can read the entry, and no one has ever come to claim them. He has never opened them. He says that is not a warehouseman's business. Mabyn, the keeper of the hall door, says they are full of Old Bain's spare boat-hooks. Hobart says Mabyn has a story for everything.

### What he notices

Lately the warehouses are emptier than they should be at this time of year. Fewer cargoes come in, and the ones that do come late. Hobart has noticed, because he counts everything, and he has started keeping a second tally on the door post: not of crates, but of ships expected and ships arrived. The gap is getting wider.

### Hooks

A quest could settle a count that Hobart and Garrow cannot agree on, find out what is in the four oldest crates and who left them, or help Hobart read the faded first page of the oldest ledger.
