Ottilie is the Clerk of the Harbour Hall, and if it happened in Bain Harbour, she has written it down. Every ship that comes and goes, every cargo landed or loaded, every harbour due paid or owed and every decision of the harbour council goes into her ledgers, in a small upright hand with no blots. She is thirty-eight, precise, tidy and fond of rules, but kinder than her ledgers suggest. On the corner of her desk stands a glass jar of sweets, and anyone who fills in a form correctly may take one. Sailors have been known to fill in a form twice for the practice.

### Learning the columns

Ottilie grew up on Merchant Street, and at fourteen she went to learn bookkeeping from Elowen, who kept the books for half the merchants on the street and whose daughter Maren is now the harbour schoolteacher. Elowen taught her that a ledger is a promise: that what is written there must be what happened, no more and no less, or the whole harbour is building on sand. Ottilie believed her completely, and still does. When the clerk's desk at the Harbour Hall fell empty fourteen years ago, Elowen recommended her, and the council took her on at twenty-four.

She served six years under the Harbour Master before Quenna and has served Quenna for eight. She likes Quenna enormously and finds her exhausting. Quenna decides things before the tide turns; Ottilie writes them down as she goes, and occasionally, very politely, reminds the council of what it decided last time.

### The hall

Ottilie's desk stands just inside the door of the Harbour Hall, under the window, where the light is best for writing. Mabyn, who keeps the door, brings her a sweet bun every morning and has decided she works too hard. They are firm friends of opposite kinds: Mabyn never finishes a sentence and Ottilie never leaves one unfinished.

She is fond of the harbour's children, though she would never say so in those words. She gave Jowan sweets for years when he ran errands through the hall, long before he was old enough to fill in a form. When Bree, the fishmonger's daughter, showed her the slate on which she keeps her crab-shell accounts, Ottilie said it was neater than half the ledgers she receives, and meant it. Now and then she walks down to the warehouses with her ledger to check her figures against Hobart's chalk tallies, because he is the only other person in the harbour who counts as carefully as she does.

### The small boats

Because she records everything, Ottilie notices things nobody else has added up. A few years ago she began, out of simple tidiness, to keep a tally of the small boats that go out past the headland: fishing boats, rowing boats, pleasure boats, anything without a cargo to declare. The Harbour Watch notes them and she copies the notes into a column of their own. Once she had started, she could not resist going back through the Watch's old notes and filling the column in for ten summers past.

The column is getting shorter. Ten summers ago, a fair week saw forty or more small boats go out past the headland. Last summer, a fair week saw twenty-two. This spring it is fewer still. Nobody has made a rule about it, and nobody has said a word in council. The fishing families have simply, one by one, stopped going out so far.

Ottilie is not sure who she should tell. A clerk records; she does not advise. The council is made of captains, shipwrights and merchants, and the small boats belong to none of them. She could tell Quenna, but Quenna would decide something at once, and Ottilie is not sure yet what there is to decide. And she knows, in the tidy part of her mind that never lies, that once she says it aloud, the column will mean something, and she will have to admit what.

### What she wants and fears

Ottilie wants the ledgers to stay true and the harbour to stay safe, and she has always believed the first would take care of the second. She fears that it will not, and that the numbers she wrote so neatly will be read one day as a warning nobody heard.

### Hooks

A quest could carry Ottilie's column of small boats to the right person, ask the fishing families why they no longer go past the headland, or find the one council decision missing from her ledgers that she has never been able to account for.
