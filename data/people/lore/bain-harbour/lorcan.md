Lorcan sings at the Salt and Bread most nights, and on a good night you can hear the chorus from the end of the outer pier. He is thirty-four, dramatic, charming and terrible with money, and he can make a whole inn of tired sailors sing along by the second verse. He stands on a chair to do it, which Delphine has asked him not to do roughly a thousand times.

### A voice from everywhere

Lorcan was born in a harbour he does not remember, the son of Pascoe, a fiddler who played on ships and in waterfront inns all along the coast of Veligor and never stayed anywhere longer than a season. Lorcan grew up in a dozen ports, sleeping under tables while his father played above them, and he learned every shanty, ballad and drinking song on the coast before he could read. Pascoe died when Lorcan was twenty, in an inn not unlike the Salt and Bread, with his fiddle across his knees. Lorcan kept the fiddle, sold it within the year to pay a debt, and has regretted it ever since.

He came to Bain Harbour at twenty-two, twelve years ago, meaning to stay a fortnight. Ysella, Delphine's mother, who kept the Salt and Bread then, heard him sing one night and offered him supper and a corner to sleep in for every night he sang. He has been singing there ever since. When Ysella died eight years ago, Delphine kept him on, for the same reason her mother did: a full room drinks more.

### The debt

Lorcan owes Delphine money. He owes it in the plain sense, a tab that runs down one whole column of the book behind her bar, and in the less plain sense of a dozen nights when she has let him off. He is not a gambler or a drunkard. He is simply generous with money he does not have: he buys rounds for crews who have had a bad voyage, lends coins to deckhands who will never repay them, and recently bought the old captain Thaddeus a drink four separate times in exchange for the story of the race Thaddeus lost by a boat's length thirty-three years ago. Thaddeus told it with a different wind each time. Lorcan used all four.

Delphine never forgets a debt or a kindness, and Lorcan is in both of her columns. He knows it, and it is the only thing in the world that makes him quiet.

### The race shanty

Lorcan is writing a new shanty about the summer race around Veligor. It has a verse for every headland on the course, a verse for the Harbour Master that makes Quenna laugh out loud, a verse for Thaddeus's lost race that makes the old man wipe his eyes, and a chorus that half the waterfront already knows. What it does not have is an ending. Lorcan needs Bain Harbour to win so that he can finish it properly, with the boat crossing the line and the whole inn on its feet.

Delphine told him to write two endings. He has written the winning ending five times. He has not written the losing one at all. He says it would be bad luck, and that Captain Annis agrees with him.

### The songs nobody asks for

Lately something has changed in the Salt and Bread, and Lorcan has felt it before anyone told him. The old sea songs about serpents and drowned bells and things in the deep used to be the ones the sailors shouted for. Now, when he starts one, a crew at the long table goes quiet, and once a captain put a hand on his arm and asked him, quite gently, to sing something else. He has stopped singing them. He has not stopped thinking about why.

### What he wants and fears

Lorcan wants one song of his own that the harbour will still be singing after he is gone, the way his father's songs are still sung in harbours that have forgotten his name. He fears that the race will be lost, the shanty will never be finished, and he will be remembered as the man who owed Delphine money.

### Hooks

A quest could help Lorcan find the ending of his shanty, buy back his father's fiddle from wherever it went, or find out which old sea song a captain asked him not to sing, and why.
