Peregrine is Dunstan's apprentice at the Bain Harbour shipyard, now in his second year. He is nineteen, clever, clumsy and full of ideas, about one in ten of them good. He talks fast when he is nervous, which around Dunstan is most of the time, and he has a habit of explaining his newest idea while walking backwards, which is how he went off the end of the slipway into the harbour in his first week.

### From the city

Peregrine grew up in Selsior, where his parents still live, and at fourteen he was apprenticed to a cabinetmaker there. He was good with wood and bad at furniture: every chair he made came out a little too much like a boat. One summer his master sent him to deliver a writing desk to a merchant in Bain Harbour, and Peregrine stood on the quay with the desk on a handcart and watched a ship being launched from Dunstan's yard. He forgot the desk for most of an afternoon. A little over a year ago, at eighteen, he finished his indenture, walked back to the coast with his tools on his back, and asked Dunstan to take him on. Dunstan said no three times. The fourth time he said Peregrine could sweep the yard and see how long he lasted.

He lasted. He sleeps in the loft above the yard's workshop, among the spare oars and the smell of pitch, and he writes to his parents every month with drawings of whatever he is building. They write back telling him to eat more.

### The yard

Dunstan treats Peregrine's ideas the way a man treats weather: he lets the boy talk until he runs out of breath, then puts him to work planing. Peregrine has proposed, in his time at the yard, a bucket that empties itself, a mast that folds in half for low bridges, a rudder worked from the bow and a sail with windows in it so the crew can see where they are going. He keeps a notebook of every idea, good and bad, and crosses out the bad ones himself, usually after Alwyn has looked at them. Alwyn, Dunstan's son, listens to all of them kindly and keeps about one in ten. Peregrine likes him very much and is slightly in awe of how much timber he can carry.

Kit, Dunstan's nine-year-old grandson, thinks Peregrine is the most interesting person in the yard after his grandfather, because Peregrine's ideas come even faster than Kit's. The two of them can talk for an hour without either finishing a sentence. Peregrine taught Kit to read a set of lines; Kit taught Peregrine two knots he did not know and laughed at him for not knowing them.

### The faster hull

Peregrine's one great idea, the one he is sure is in the good tenth, is a faster hull for the summer race around Veligor. It is longer and narrower than the boats Dunstan builds, with a finer entry at the bow and a flatter run under the stern, so that in a good breeze it would lift and skim instead of pushing the sea aside. He has drawn it a dozen times and carved a half-model of it from a single block of pear wood. With Kit's help he has tested it against a model of the old shape in the rock pools under the harbour wall, towing both on strings, and his model won every time but one, when Kit stepped on it.

The half-model sits on the end of Dunstan's workbench under a rag, where Peregrine left it two months ago. Dunstan has not lifted the rag. He is building his own new race boat this summer, the plans for which he keeps rolled in oilcloth under his bed and will not show to anyone. Peregrine has never tried to look at them. He has thought about it. He would like it noted that he has not.

### What he wants and fears

Peregrine wants to see a boat built to his lines cross the finish line first, and he wants Dunstan to be the one who builds it. He fears that the old man will never lift the rag, and that he himself is one of the nine bad ideas and has not noticed yet.

### Hooks

A quest could race Peregrine's model against the old shape in front of witnesses, get Dunstan to lift the rag on his workbench, or help Peregrine fetch a length of pear wood good enough for a full-sized trial hull.
