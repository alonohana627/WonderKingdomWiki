Halloran is the captain of the Harbour Watch, and he keeps the peace on the Bain Harbour waterfront mostly by being impossible to rattle. He is forty-seven, steady, warm and hard to surprise. He settles most trouble with a hand on the shoulder and a mug of something hot, and sailors who were shouting a moment earlier find themselves sitting down and explaining their side of it to him instead.

### The Salt and Bread

Halloran grew up at the Salt and Bread, the waterfront inn his grandmother opened in an old net loft and his mother, Ysella, kept after her. His father, Edwy, was a sailor who was lost at sea in a winter storm when Halloran was twelve, long before anyone talked about creatures in the deep water. After that Halloran was the one who carried the drunks home and sorted out the fights in the taproom, while his younger sister Delphine grew up carrying mugs behind the bar. He learned there everything he knows about keeping the peace: that most trouble is tiredness, that most tired people want to be listened to, and that a warm drink settles more arguments than a raised voice.

Ysella died eight years ago and left the inn to Delphine. Halloran still drinks there most evenings after his rounds, at the end of the bar nearest the door, and he and his sister have looked after each other ever since. They are very much alike. She keeps the peace from behind the bar, he keeps it in front of it, and between them very little trouble on the waterfront gets as far as a broken chair.

### The Watch

Halloran joined the Harbour Watch at twenty-two and has been its captain for eleven years. The Watch is small: a handful of watchmen and watchwomen who walk the docks and the Waterfront, check moorings, keep an eye on the warehouses and break up arguments between sailors and tired dockworkers. It also keeps a launch, built by Dunstan the shipwright, for fetching people out of the water.

He treats every watchman exactly the same, and that includes Jowan, the Harbour Master's son, who joined last spring and salutes everybody. Halloran is fond of the boy and has quietly noticed that his reports, though far too long, are the most careful in the Watch. He is fond too of Brisa, who joined at the same time, is the fastest runner on the waterfront and never lets Jowan forget it. Their rivalry makes both of them better, and Halloran sees no reason to stop it.

### The outer pier

This spring Halloran began posting a watch on the outer pier at night, and he will only say it is a precaution. The truth is something he has told only the Harbour Master. Early one morning this spring he walked to the end of the outer pier at low tide and found the Watch's own launch hanging from a mooring line pulled taut straight down into the water, and long fresh grooves scored into the pilings below the waterline, deeper than any boat could have made. By the time he had fetched help, the line had gone slack and the water was still.

Since then he has kept men on the pier every night and stood most of those watches himself. He has asked Garrow to stop unloading at the end of the pier after dark. Jowan has asked three times for the outer pier watch, and Halloran has said no three times, not because the boy is not good enough, but because Quenna is the one person who knows what is under that water, and he will not stand her son over it. Delphine knows it is more than a precaution. He has not told her, and she has not asked, which is how he knows she knows.

### What he wants and fears

Halloran wants the waterfront to stay the kind of place where the worst trouble is a sailor who has had too much to drink. He fears that one night the watch on the outer pier will see what made those grooves, and that a hand on the shoulder will not settle it.

He has not yet asked Brisa for the list of strange things she has been keeping. He should.

### Hooks

A quest could stand a night on the outer pier with Halloran, dive under the pier at low tide to look at the pilings, or bring together the people in the harbour who have each been quietly writing down what the sea is doing.
