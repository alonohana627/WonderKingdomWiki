Bethoc is forty-five and keeps the Gap Inn on the Lantern Market, where sailors, hunters, drovers and travellers all end up sooner or later. She is warm, quick-witted and unshockable. She has heard every sailor's story twice, and the second time she usually corrects the details. Her stew is the best on the quay, her ale is honest, and her rooms are small, clean and nearly always full.

### The inn

The Gap Inn is a long, low timber house with a stone hearth at one end and a view of the inlet from the other, and Bethoc was born in the room above the kitchen. Her father, Malcolm, kept the inn for thirty-five years before her, and her mother died when she was a baby, so Bethoc grew up behind the bar, carrying bowls before she could see over it. Malcolm died eleven years ago, and the inn has been hers since. Some of the old regulars still call it "Malcolm's," and she lets them.

Her day starts before dawn with the stew pot and ends long after dark with the last singer. In between she pours, cooks, mends quarrels, sends drunk sailors back to their ships, and listens. People tell Bethoc things. She has never been sure why, but she knows more about Goa Harbour than anyone except perhaps Hamish at the rope lift, and unlike Hamish she keeps most of it to herself.

### The board

Five years ago Bethoc grew tired of hunters arguing at her tables about where a creature had been seen, and on what night, and by whom. She nailed a broad plank to the wall by the door and chalked the sightings on it herself: what, where, when and who said so. It has been there ever since. Now the hunters check it every morning before they go out. Torva reads it in silence and leaves; Kenna reads it and complains that nothing on it is big enough. Fishers, drovers and timber cutters add to it, and Bethoc wipes off old sightings once a month and keeps a tally of how many there were in a little book under the bar.

The tally is the part nobody else sees, and it is the part that worries her. Each year the book needs more pages. She has begun to show it, quietly, to the people who might do something with it.

### The people of the inn

Cormac, the old shanty singer, has had the same seat by the fire for longer than Bethoc has kept the inn. He sings the songs of the shared camp, weeps at his own verses, and flatters her shamelessly in the hope of a free cup, which he usually gets. Mhairi, his old pupil, plays the fiddle for Bethoc most nights and at the summer race send-off, and plays faster whenever people try to stop dancing. Bethoc pays her in coin, stew, and the right to play whatever she likes.

And then there is Brodie, the fishmonger, Bethoc's sweetheart for the past three years. He is loud where she is dry, and he names every fish he sells, which she pretends to find ridiculous. He comes in every evening after the market closes and sits at the end of the bar until she has time for him, however long it takes. Neither of them has said anything about marrying, and the whole Lantern Market has opinions about that.

### What she wants

Bethoc wants her inn full, her people fed and her board empty, and she suspects she will only ever get two of the three. She fears the night when one of the hunters who reads her board in the morning does not come back to the inn in the evening.

### Hooks

The sighting board is a natural place for a quest to begin: a strange entry chalked by someone nobody saw come in, or a creature that turns up on it three nights running in the same spot. Bethoc might also ask someone to carry her tally book to the Wardens' House, if she could be sure they would not read it on the way.
