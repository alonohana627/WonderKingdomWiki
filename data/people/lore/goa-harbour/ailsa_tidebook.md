Ailsa is seventy-three and the Keeper of the Tide Book, the great ledger in the Wardens' House where the sea wardens of Goa Harbour have written down every tide since the first camp on the shore. She is precise, dry and quietly delighted by patterns. Ask her about the spring tide of a certain storm forty years ago and she will tell you the hour of the turn, the height on the quay steps, the wind, and which warden wrote it down, and then she will tell you he spelled the wind wrong.

### The book and its keeper

The Tide Book is not really one book any more. Its oldest pages, from the first camp, are a mixture of Savage tally marks and a careful Magician hand, and they have been rebound so many times that the spine is newer than the wardens who use it. Later volumes stand beside it on a long shelf in a dry room at the back of the Wardens' House, and the current one lies open on a slanted desk under the window. Every day the wardens bring Ailsa their tides: the hour of high and low water, the strength of the run through the gap, the eddies, the weather. She writes them in, checks them against the same moon in every year before, and asks sharp questions when something does not fit.

Ailsa came to the Wardens' House at seventeen, as copyist to old Keeper Innes, in the same spring that Varro became a warden. She has been keeper herself since she was twenty-seven, forty-six years now, and she and Varro are the last two left from those early days. He trusts her memory more than his own. She trusts his eyes more than her own. Between them, they used to say, they made one very good warden.

Her closest friend is Halvard, the clerk of the Wardens' House, who records the ships while she records the water. They eat their midday bread together at her desk most days. He cannot bear the crooked stack of loose notes she keeps at her elbow and straightens it every time he passes, and she puts it crooked again as soon as he is gone, because watching him try not to notice is one of the small pleasures of her day.

### The pages that do not match

For the past few years the new pages have not matched the old ones as well as they should. It is nothing a passing reader would see. The spring highs run a hand's breadth higher than the same moons did in the past. The slack water at the turn comes a few minutes late, then a few more. The strongest run through the gap has drifted, very slightly, toward the cliff on the forest side. Any one of these could be a careless warden or a strange year. All of them together, year on year, are a pattern, and patterns are what Ailsa understands best.

She has not said so out loud. She is trying to work out why before she does, because a keeper who cries out without a reason is no use to anyone. Iska, the First Warden, has seen the pages herself and asked her once what they meant. Ailsa told her, "Not yet." She has noticed that Varro, who always asks about anything odd in the water, has not asked her about this at all, and she suspects that means he has felt it too and is afraid of the answer. She is a little afraid of it herself.

### What she fears

Ailsa fears that the water she has spent her life writing down is turning into water she does not know. She fears, more privately, that the answer is already somewhere in the oldest pages, in a hand she can only half read.

### Hooks

A quest might carry tide marks to Ailsa from somewhere along the coast that the wardens do not reach, help her compare the oldest pages with what the coves and the cliffs remember, or find someone who can read the Magician hand of the first camp.
