Teague is fifty and a highland drover, the man who brings Morag's wool and the highland trappers' pelts down from the hills to Goa Harbour by the hill road. He is easygoing, weathered and full of stories that grow with each telling. Ask him about the hill road and he will tell you about the time he was snowed into a shepherd's bothy for nine days with a goat that could count. Ask him again next year and it will be twelve days, and the goat will have learned to play dice.

### The hill road

Teague was born in a herding hamlet high in the hills, where the grass gives out into rock and the wind never quite stops. He has walked the hill road down to the coast since he was a boy driving sheep behind his uncle, long dead now, and he has driven his own flocks on it for thirty years. He knows every bend, every ford, every spring and every bothy along it, and the narrow stretch near the top where the road crosses between two crags and the shepherds call it the Saddle, because it is the one place where a flock has to go single file.

For the last twenty-two of those years he has driven for Morag, the wool trader of the Lantern Market. He brings down her wool and fleeces after the shearing, takes back salt, tools and news, and spends a week or two in Goa Harbour between trips, mostly at the Gap Inn, telling stories to anyone who will buy him a cup. Morag feeds him every time he comes to her stall, scolds him for being thin, and worries about him every time he takes the road. He pretends not to notice. He would be lost without her, and she knows it.

### Last spring

The hill road was always rough, but it was never deadly. Last spring it was. Teague was bringing a flock down with two herd-dogs and a hired boy when something came out of the rocks at the Saddle at dusk and went through the sheep in the narrow place where they could not scatter. By morning he had lost half the flock. Nobody was killed, though one of the dogs has never been the same, and the hired boy went back up into the hills and did not come down again.

When Teague tells this at the Gap Inn, the creature is twice the size of a cart, has too many legs and breathes smoke. When he tells it to Morag, it is shorter, and he does not tell her the part where he lay behind a rock until dawn listening to it. When he tells it to nobody, late at night, he admits that he never really saw it at all.

### Looking for company

The next trip is due after the summer shearing, and Teague will not take the road again without brave company. He has asked Esk, the sellsword from the Inlet Quay, to guard the flock. She has not said yes, and she has not said no, and Teague has decided this is the most encouraging answer he has ever had. He has also asked half the adventurers at the Gap Inn, and most of them have laughed and said they would come if he paid them in stories. He is not sure they were joking. He would pay them anyway.

### What he wants and fears

Teague wants the hill road to be the road he grew up on again: long and cold and dull and safe. He fears it never will be, and that the highland trappers and herders, who already come down less every year, will one day stop coming at all. He fears, too, that Morag will be the one waiting at the stall the day he does not come back, and that is the story he never tells.

### Hooks

A quest might guard Teague's flock on the hill road through the Saddle, go up ahead of him to find out what is waiting in the rocks there, or persuade Esk to give him an answer at last.
