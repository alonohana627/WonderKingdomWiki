Dougal is sixty-six and the night watchman of the Inlet Quay, and he has walked it from dusk to first light for thirty years. He counts the lanterns, he counts the boats, he checks the mooring lines and the warehouse doors, and then he starts again from the other end. He looks permanently half asleep, with heavy eyelids and a slow, rolling walk, and he is not. He can hear a rope creak against the wrong bollard from the far end of the quay. To keep himself awake he tells jokes, terrible ones, to the gulls, the boats, the lanterns and anyone foolish enough to stop and talk.

### Before the watch

Dougal grew up on the Inlet Quay and went to sea young as a deckhand on the timber ships, hauling cargo out through the gap behind the wardens' pilot boats. He liked the work and hated the seasickness, which never left him in twenty years of sailing. In his early thirties he came ashore for good and worked as a quay porter, and at thirty-six, when old Ewart, the night watchman, decided his legs had walked enough nights, Dougal asked for the lantern and the stick. Ewart handed them over with a single piece of advice: count everything, every night, because one night the count will be wrong. Dougal has counted ever since.

He never married. He lives in a little stone watch-hut at the end of the quay, near the foot of the Long Stair, and sleeps there through the day with a blanket over the window. He says the quay is the only family that never asks him where he has been.

### The night quay

Dougal's evening begins when Dunmor the lamplighter lights the last lantern and ends when Dunmor comes back at first light to put them out. In between, Dougal knows every sound the quay makes. He knows which boats knock in a swell and which grind, which warehouse door rattles in a north wind, and which of the lanterns burns a shade lower than its neighbours. He has seen a great many things on the quay at night that were nobody's business, and he has told none of them. He has, for instance, seen the First Warden's son Arlo slipping down to the quay at dawn as he goes off duty, and has said nothing about it, which in Goa Harbour seems to be the custom.

At the change of the night watch he walks to the bottom of the stairs and shares a pot of tea with Ruan, the captain of the Cliff Watch. They have done it for years. Ruan was a new recruit once, falling asleep on his feet at his post, and Dougal kept him awake with jokes until his watch was over. Ruan says the jokes were so bad he stayed awake out of fury. Dougal says that is the whole point.

His favourite, which he tells at least once a week: why did the gull sit on the lantern? Because it wanted to be a light-house bird. Nobody has ever laughed. Dougal laughs every time.

### The tracks

Early this spring, on his last round before first light, Dougal found wet tracks on the lowest steps of the quay, where the stone runs down into the water. They were wide and splayed, with the marks of claws at the ends of long toes, and they came up out of the sea onto the bottom steps and went back down again. They were wider than his boot. Since then he has found them on about a dozen mornings, never much higher than the bottom few steps, always drying fast. He measures them against his boot, and then he sluices them away with a bucket of seawater before the quay wakes, so that nobody takes fright.

He has told only Ruan. He trusts Ruan to know what to do with it, and he does not trust himself to tell it without making it a joke. Once or twice this spring, on a still night, he has also seen the lanterns on the lowest landing dip all together and come back. He assumed Dunmor knew. Neither of them has mentioned it to the other.

### What he wants and fears

Dougal wants to walk the quay until his legs go, as Ewart did, and hand the lantern to someone who will count. He fears the night his count will be wrong, and he has begun, for the first time in thirty years, to keep his back to the wall when he passes the lowest steps.

### Hooks

A quest might sit up with Dougal on the lowest steps of the quay through a night to see what leaves the tracks, or help him carry his measurements up to the Wardens' House when Ruan finally decides to speak. Another might begin on the morning his count of boats comes up one short.
