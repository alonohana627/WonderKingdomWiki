Agna is seventy-two and the net-mender of the Fishers' Coves, and she has kept the coves' nets whole for fifty years. Every boat in the coves has fished with her mending, and every fisher in the coves has heard what she thinks of their knots. She is blunt, wry and tireless. She sits on an upturned crate at the top of the shingle with a net across her knees and a wooden needle in her fist, and she does not stop working to talk, or to laugh, or to tell you that the knot you tied this morning would shame a seal.

### A cove girl

Agna was born in the coves, the daughter of a cove fisher named Niall and a net-mender named Kirsty, and she learned to fill a needle before she could read. Her mother taught her to mend in the evenings by the fire, holding up each knot to the light and pulling it until it either held or gave. When Kirsty's eyes began to fail, Agna, at twenty-two, took her place on the crate. She has not given it up since.

When Agna was ten there came a spring tide higher than anyone in the coves remembered. Her father lifted her onto the rocks at the top of the flood and let her cut a notch with his gutting knife to mark where the water stood. She still tells the story, usually to grandchildren who have heard it before, and the notch is still there on the cove rocks. Agna has not walked out to look at it in years. Her knees prefer the crate.

### The calm years

Agna remembers when the sea was calm enough to fish at night. When she was young the cove boats went out on still summer nights with a lantern in every bow, and the girls on the shore could count them strung out across the water like a second line of stars. She went out herself, more than once, with her friend Maili, who later married Varro the warden. The night fishing thinned through the years when Sorcha was growing up, and nobody in the coves has fished at night for more than twenty years. Agna tells the young ones about it as if it were a fairy tale. Some of them think it is.

### Family

Agna married Donal, a cove fisher, a big slow man with large careful hands who never once tied a knot she could fault, which she says is the only reason she married him. He died of a winter fever fifteen years ago. Their daughter Sorcha has his boat, the Sea Thrift, and has patched it so often that Agna says there is more mending in it than boat. Sorcha is as loud and stubborn as her mother, which is why they argue every day, and why neither could do without it.

Agna approves of Calder, her son-in-law, mostly because he mends things without being asked. She taught her granddaughter Eilidh knots before letters, and has never yet told her a knot was better than "not bad", because the girl would stop trying. Her grandson Ronan she calls "the magpie", and she saves him the bright things that come up snagged in the nets: sea-glass, a brass button, once a little bell. She also has an opinion about the bridge knots of Kestra, whose mother Shona was a cove net-maker. Agna says they are "Shona's knots, near enough", which from Agna is a medal.

### The torn nets

These last few years Agna has begun to find nets torn by something with teeth far bigger than any fish she knows. Not the ragged holes a seal leaves, or the clean cuts of a rock, but long bites through a dozen meshes at once, always from the boats that went out past the point. Calder has measured some of the tears with his hands. Sorcha has stopped taking the children past the point. Agna mends every one and says nothing in front of Eilidh, who notices.

Last month a broken point of something hard and pale, curved, about as long as her finger, came up caught in the mesh of one of those nets. It is not bone she knows, or shell. She keeps it wrapped in a rag at the bottom of her mending basket and has shown it to no one, not even Calder.

### What she wants and fears

Agna wants the coves' nets to come back whole. She wants to live long enough to tell Eilidh her knots are good. She fears she is mending nets for a sea that is no longer the one she learned on, and that one day the thing that bites them will not let go of the boat.

### Hooks

A quest might carry Agna's finger-long fragment to someone who could say what it came from, or go out past the point with a net of her mending to see what tears it. Another might begin with Agna asking, gruffly, for someone to walk out to her old notch on the cove rocks and tell her where the water is.
