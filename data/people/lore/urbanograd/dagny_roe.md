Dagny Roe keeps the gate on the night watch, and she carries two lamps. One hangs from her belt and one from her hand, both trimmed, both full, both lit before the sun is properly down. She carries two because one went out once. Everyone at the Gate Walk calls her Two-Lamps, and she has stopped minding. She is twenty-one, jumpy and sharp-eyed, and she talks: to the other guards, to the gate, to the moths, to the dark itself. It keeps the dark from getting the last word.

### From the harbour

Dagny grew up in Bain Harbour, on the Waterfront, where her family has rendered lamp oil from fish for three generations and sold it to every lamp, lantern and galley stove along the quay. Her parents and her two older brothers are still there, stirring the vats, and they write to her twice a year, mostly about the weather. Dagny was meant to stir the vats too. She was good at it, and could not bear the smell or the sameness, and at eighteen she came inland to see the city everyone sang about. She meant to stay a summer. She has stayed three years.

She found work hauling oil for the lamp-sellers of the Night Market, and a year later, when Brindle stood up at the Open Table asking for volunteers for the Gate Watch, Dagny surprised herself by putting her hand up. Brindle gave her the night watch, because nobody else wanted it, and because a girl who had trimmed and filled every kind of lamp on the Waterfront already knew more about keeping a flame alive in the wind than anyone else in the Watch.

### The night the lamp went out

In her first month on the gate, on a wet night in late autumn, her lamp guttered and died, and for a long minute she stood in pitch darkness with the road in front of her and something moving on it. It turned out to be a lost goat. She has never forgiven it. The next evening she came to the gate with two lamps, and she has carried two ever since.

### What she has seen

This year Dagny has seen eyes in the dark beyond the road more often than last year. Pairs of them, low in the brush, catching the lamplight and holding still. Some nights they come close enough to the walls that she can hear them breathing. She reports every sighting to Brindle, who chalks them on the weekly slate by the gate, and she has noticed the numbers only go one way.

What Dagny has not noticed is that she is the one who never steps back. When something moves out there, she raises both lamps, steps forward to the edge of the light and calls out to it, talking all the while. She thinks this is because she is too frightened to think of anything else. Brindle has seen it, and thinks it is courage, and is trying to find a way to tell her without frightening her off. Knox, who has kept the gate for ten years, brings her a carved wooden owl now and then to keep her company, and has started to call her the bravest of them, which she assumes is a joke.

### Patch

Patch, the herbalist of the Gate Walk, is Dagny's friend. She tends travelers who stumble in through the gate with monster bites and scrapes, and on bad nights Dagny is the one who brings them to her door. Patch scolds the patients while bandaging them and scolds Dagny for standing too near the road, and then makes her a cup of something hot and bitter that Dagny swears is the only reason she survives until dawn. In return Dagny walks Patch home after late patients, two lamps lighting the way.

### What she wants and fears

Dagny wants to see what the eyes belong to, and in daylight, please. She fears the night both lamps go out, and, more than that, the night something comes out of the dark that talking will not stop.

A quest might stand the night watch beside Dagny, track whatever leaves its prints by the road in the morning, or gather the herbs Patch needs from fields Dagny can see from the gate but cannot leave it to reach.
