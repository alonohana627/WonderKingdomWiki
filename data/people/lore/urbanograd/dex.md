Dex has played the pompous grandee in Juna Marrow's play for fifteen years, and every Walkout Day the whole square waits for his entrance the way children wait for snow. He is forty-one, big and booming, a born clown with a voice that can rattle the shutters on the far side of Lantern Square and an ego to match. He is also the most generous actor in the Old Barracks Troupe. He will take a laugh anywhere he can find one, and then hand it straight to whoever is standing next to him.

### The part

The grandee is the villain of Juna's comedy: a puffed-up Selsior worthy who bans everything fun, falls off things and is finally chased off the cart by the market crowd. It is the second-best part in the play, and Dex would argue the best. Old Bellamy Stroud, who directed the troupe for decades, cast him in it fifteen years ago, when Dex was twenty-six and had been playing the back end of a mule for three seasons. Two years later Bellamy died and Dina Finch took over the troupe, and she inherited Dex the way one inherits a beloved, noisy dog. They shout at each other at every rehearsal and would walk into fire for each other.

Dex knows every line of every part in the play, not just his own, and mouths along with the whole thing from the wings. When Rue won the part of Juna this spring, he took her aside and taught her where the laughs fall, how to wait for them and how to stand so the grandee's hat does not block her face. Thimble keeps that hat, an absurd tower of purple felt and feathers, locked away between shows, and Dex has never once been allowed to take it home.

### Sprout

Dex's son, Sprout, plays the market urchin who cheers for Juna in the first scene, and steals the show every year. He is eight, giggly, loud and never still, and he has learned every part in the play by accident, from growing up backstage. He wants to play the grandee one day so that he can wear the hat. Dex pretends to be outraged by the ambition and is secretly the proudest man in Urbanograd.

Sprout's mother, Lark, sang in the troupe's chorus and died of a fever when Sprout was barely a year old. Dex has raised the boy since among costume baskets and painted flats, with half the troupe as aunts and uncles, and has never once let him see his father sad in daylight. On the anniversary he takes Sprout up to the Rooftops to watch the lanterns come on, and they sing one of her songs, badly, together.

### A serious role

What Dex wants, just once, is to play something serious. Not a grandee, not a fool, not a man who falls off a cart. A grieving king, perhaps, or an old soldier, or a father who has lost something. He has learned the great tragic speeches by heart and recites them to the empty theatre in the early mornings. Nobody will take him seriously. The moment he steps on stage, audiences start smiling in anticipation, and the moment he opens his mouth they laugh, whatever comes out of it.

Dina knows he wants it. She has not yet found a part he could play without the audience laughing, and she worries about telling him so. Dex suspects she worries, and that hurts more than a refusal would.

### What he wants and fears

Dex wants one night in which a whole audience goes silent because of him. He fears he is only ever funny, and that Sprout will grow up thinking his father was nothing but a hat.

A quest might find Dex a serious play worth staging, carry a script to the Old Barracks from a writer in another town, or help him rehearse in secret somewhere nobody will laugh.
