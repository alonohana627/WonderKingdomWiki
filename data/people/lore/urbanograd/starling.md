Starling lives upside down to the rest of Urbanograd. When the Night Market is closing and the last fiddlers are packing up, Starling is wide awake on a flat roof not far from the Crooked Tower, eye to a brass tube, murmuring numbers into the dark. When the city is at its busiest in the middle of the afternoon, Starling is asleep, usually in a hammock slung between two chimney stacks, with a notebook open on Starling's chest. Starling is thirty-four, distracted, kind and wildly enthusiastic about anything in the sky, and will explain a comet to anyone who stands still for long enough, including people who were only trying to get past.

### How it started

Starling was born in Urbanograd and was the sort of child who fell asleep on the roof and had to be carried in. Starling's parents, a pair of quiet bookbinders, are both dead now; what they left was a narrow house with a flat roof and a shelf of old almanacs, one of them a battered star chart drawn by somebody in the city's early years, with the constellations given Walker names nobody else uses: the Cart, the Fiddle, the Open Door. Starling learned the sky from that chart and from lying on the slates, and has spent the years since correcting it, adding to it, and growing fonder of it with every mistake found.

The telescope came later. It is built out of Tinsel's spare lenses: the slightly wrong ones, the ones with a bubble near the edge, the ones Tinsel held up to the sun and decided were not quite good enough to sell. Tinsel, the glassblower on Workshops Row, gives them to Starling instead of melting them down, and over the years they have become a long brass tube, three eyepieces and a finder made from a bottle bottom. Tinsel comes up to look through it on clear nights and talks about the colour of starlight for hours, which suits Starling perfectly. They are friends of the kind who can lose a whole evening agreeing about something beautiful.

### The flickers

About two years ago, on a clear night with no moon, Starling noticed something wrong with the horizon. Not the sky: the very edge of the land, toward the middle of the island, where the distant country meets the stars. For a few breaths it seemed to waver, the way air wavers above a hot stove, though the night was cold. Then it was still again. Starling thought it was tired eyes. It happened again a month later, and again.

Starling has started writing down the dates, and the hours, and the weather, without knowing what any of them mean. The flickers are not regular. They do not follow the moon or the seasons, as far as Starling can tell. They do not show on every clear night, and they never show from anywhere in the city lower than the Rooftops.

Perch, who keeps pigeons on the Crooked Tower and is Starling's friend, keeps a list of the routes the birds have begun to refuse. Last autumn Starling brought the notebook of dates up to the loft for the first time, and now the two of them sit late at night with their lists side by side, comparing. Some of the days seem to match. Some do not. Neither of them knows what that means, and neither has yet quite said aloud what they are both wondering.

### What Starling wants and fears

Starling wants to understand the flickers, and also, more simply, to finish the chart: every star over Urbanograd named and placed, in a book anyone could use. Starling fears that the flickers mean something everyone ought to know about, and that by the time anyone believes a person who sleeps all afternoon, it will be too late to matter.

A quest might watch the horizon with Starling on a night of flickers, carry Starling's dates and Perch's routes to someone who might read them, or bring Tinsel's newest lens safely up through the crowded streets.
