Piper writes the songs for the Old Barracks Troupe, and is never not humming one. Piper is twenty, thin and untidy, with ink on both hands, a tin whistle in one pocket and a stub of pencil behind each ear, because one always goes missing. Piper is romantic, scatterbrained and helplessly in love with music, and falls in love with a new melody about once a week: a tune whistled by a carter, the rhythm of a loose shutter, the creak of the theatre cart as it turns. Each one is the most beautiful thing Piper has ever heard, until the next.

### Songs for the play

Juna Marrow's play has been performed on a cart every Walkout Day for a hundred years, and the words have barely changed. The songs are another matter. The troupe has always let them drift with the times, and for the last four years they have been Piper's. Every year Piper rewrites them: new tunes, new verses, new jokes about whatever the city has argued about at the Open Table that spring. Dina Finch, the director, despairs. She cuts half of them in rehearsal, threatens to cut the rest, and then hums the survivors all summer when she thinks nobody is listening. Piper has heard her and has never said a word, which may be the most tactful thing Piper has ever done.

The crowd loves the new songs. By the end of Walkout Day half the square is singing them, and by the autumn the Night Market is still singing them, slightly wrong. Hambone, who keeps the cart, hums the old versions at the same time, on purpose, and the two of them have a long, friendly war over which ones the city will remember. Sprout, Dex's son, knows all of them, old and new, and sings them in whichever order annoys both sides most.

### Where it began

Piper grew up in Urbanograd, in the narrow streets behind the Old Barracks, and began hanging about the theatre at twelve, carrying ladders and listening at the doors. Piper learned to read music from scraps and to play the whistle from anyone who would put up with it. Ozzie Lantry, the historian, once sang Piper the old Walker songs his grandfather had taught him, very badly, and Piper wrote every one of them down and still keeps them, tied with a ribbon. At sixteen Piper played a new tune for the grandee's entrance during a rehearsal, without being asked; Dina shouted, then laughed, and then kept it. That was the beginning.

### Saffi

There is one melody Piper has loved for longer than a week. Saffi, the fiddler of Lantern Square, plays everything twice as fast as anyone else and is writing a tune for every street in the city. Piper has heard all nine she has finished, from the back of the crowd, more times than can be counted, and has written a second part for each of them, to be played on the whistle underneath her fiddle. Piper wants, more than anything, to play them with her. Piper has not asked. Every evening Piper walks to the edge of the square with the whistle, listens to Saffi and Drum play until the lanterns are bright, and walks home again without saying a word.

### What Piper wants and fears

Piper wants to hear one song of Piper's own sung by the whole square on Walkout Day, and to play one tune, just one, beside Saffi. Piper fears that the moment Saffi hears the whistle part, she will laugh, and not in the kind way.

A quest might carry Piper's written-out second parts to Saffi, find a lost Walker song for the play, or help Piper rescue a favourite song from Dina's cuts.
