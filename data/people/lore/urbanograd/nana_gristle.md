Nana Gristle sits on the same bench on the sunny side of Lantern Square every day of the year, rain or shine, from mid-morning until the lanterns come on. She is seventy-nine, tiny, wrapped in three shawls in all weathers, and sharp enough to cut bread with. She knows everyone's grandparents, and everything their grandparents ever did that they would rather forget, and she is happy to share it. Nothing shocks her. She has seen the city do all of it before, usually on a Walkout Day, usually on a roof.

Nobody is quite sure when she became Nana. She has no grandchildren and never had children of her own; the square simply started calling her that some forty years ago, and Gristle came with it, because she is tough as gristle and twice as hard to get rid of. She outlived a husband and two sweethearts and speaks fondly of all three, though never in the same breath.

### The basket

Nana Gristle's mother, Posy, was carried out of Selsior in a basket on the day of the Long Walk. Posy was a baby then, a few months old, and her mother, a lace-maker called Marguerite who had never been allowed into a guild, carried her the whole way in a laundry basket on one hip, with her lace bobbins packed in around the baby to keep her warm. Posy grew up in the roofless fortress among the first Walkers and lived to be very old, and died eighteen years ago still able to recite which songs her mother sang on the road.

Nana treats this as the most important fact in the city, and in a way she is right: Posy was one of the very last people alive who had been on the Long Walk, and through her, Nana is one of the very last people who heard about it first-hand. Nana tells the story to anyone who sits on her bench for longer than a minute. Ozzie Lantry, the city's historian, has heard it so often that he could recite it, and still writes it down every time in case she adds something. Sometimes she does. Ozzie suspects some of the additions are new; Nana says the basket was hers to remember, not his.

### The bench

From her bench Nana watches everything. She tells newcomers which rooftops to avoid, and she is always right, because she knows which ones fell in during her childhood, which were patched badly afterwards, and by whom. Kite, the rooftop runner, finds this deeply irritating. She judges Figgy's chalk drawings with a critic's eye and keeps a honey cake in her pocket for the good ones. She scolds Rook, the Caller of the Open Table, every time Rook passes, for skipping meals, for speaking too softly and for letting Bastian go on, and Rook stops to listen anyway.

Saucer, who has kept the tea stall at the Night Market for forty years, is Nana's oldest friend. Each evening, as the market opens, Nana leaves her bench and walks across for a cup of spiced tea and a honey cake, and the two of them sit with their heads together, saying terrible things about everyone and laughing at their own jokes. Saucer reads her leaves every evening and always predicts something nice; Nana always says it is nonsense, and always asks what it said.

### What she wants and fears

Nana Gristle wants the city to remember where it came from, and to remember that it came from people carrying babies and bobbins, not from heroes. She fears, though she would never say so, that when she goes, the basket goes with her.

A quest might find one of Marguerite's lace pieces in an old attic, help Ozzie test which parts of Nana's story are new, or fetch something sweet enough to bribe a story out of her.
