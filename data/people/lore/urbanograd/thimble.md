Thimble has pins in her mouth. She has had pins in her mouth, as far as anyone at the Old Barracks can remember, for thirty years, and in all that time she has never once stopped talking around them. She is sixty-six, small, sharp-eyed and fussy, with spectacles on a ribbon, a tape measure round her neck and a pincushion strapped to her wrist like a bracer. She can tell from across the stage that a hem has dropped by the width of a fingernail, and she will say so, through the pins, during the most moving speech in the play.

### Coming to the troupe

Thimble was born in Urbanograd and grew up sewing. For years she worked for a dressmaker off the Night Market, turning out party clothes for half the city, and on Walkout Days she stood in the crowd and watched the troupe's costumes come apart at the seams in front of everyone. One year she went backstage after the play to tell them so. The troupe's costume maker then, an old woman called Wilhelmina Gage, listened to the whole complaint, handed her a needle and a split doublet, and said that if she knew so much she could start now. Thimble was thirty-six. Wilhelmina died a few winters later, and the baskets were Thimble's.

That was thirty years ago, back when Bellamy Stroud still directed, and Thimble has sewn every costume the troupe has worn since: the market crowd's patched coats, Juna's famous green jacket, the chorus's ribbons and the absurd, glorious, feathered nonsense of the grandee. She keeps them in wicker baskets labelled in a hand nobody else can read, and she knows which patch on which coat came from which year's play.

### The hat

The grandee's hat is her particular charge: a tall tower of purple felt and feathers, rebuilt a little every spring, that gets the biggest laugh in the play before Dex has even opened his mouth. Thimble keeps it locked away between shows, in a box with a key she wears on the same ribbon as her spectacles. Dex has asked to take it home every year for fifteen years. She has said no every year, and she will go on saying no, because she knows exactly what Dex would do with it at the Night Market.

Little Sprout, Dex's son, wants to play the grandee one day so that he can wear that hat. Thimble tells him it would sit on his shoulders. She has also, without telling anybody, started keeping the feathers she trims off each spring in a separate packet, in case a smaller hat is ever needed.

### Friends and fabric

Hambone, who keeps the cart, is her oldest friend. They have shared a bench backstage for thirty years, Thimble sewing and Hambone polishing, and they argue about everything: feathers, the speed of the Walkers' song, whether the cart needs a new coat of paint. She knows about the cracked wheel, and has not said a word to anyone, because Hambone's ears go red whenever it comes up and she cannot bear to watch. For two years Rue fetched Thimble's pins and swept her scraps before anyone knew Rue could act. Thimble is now telling everyone that she knew all along. She did not.

Thimble buys her brightest fabric from Dyna, the tailor on Workshops Row who dyes cloth in colours no guild would allow. She complains about the price every single time, at length, with pins, and Dyna compliments her spectacles and does not move a copper. Both of them enjoy it enormously. Lately the cloth from outside the walls has grown scarcer, and Dyna's prices have risen for real, and the haggling has a sharper edge than it used to.

### What she wants and fears

Thimble is secretly sentimental. She has kept a scrap from every Juna jacket she ever made, sewn into the lining of her own coat. She wants the troupe to look splendid on the cart every Walkout Day for as long as she can thread a needle, and she fears the year her eyes are not good enough to see the dropped hem, and nobody tells her.

A quest might bring a bolt of good cloth safely through the roads, find a lost basket of costumes before the play, or help Thimble track down the oldest Juna jacket, which she has never been able to find.
