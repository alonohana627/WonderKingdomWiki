Osmund is seventy, the ferryman of the Riverside, and he has poled the ferry across the river since he was a boy of twelve. In fifty-eight years he has said, by his daughter's count, perhaps a thousand words to his passengers, most of them "mind the edge". He speaks mostly in nods. There is a nod for "get in", a nod for "sit down", a nod for "you are welcome" and a nod for "that will be a copper, and I can see you have one". The Riverside folk understand every one of them. Strangers learn fast.

### The river's man

His father kept the ferry before him, and taught him the way Osmund later taught his own daughter: by doing it in front of him until he could do it himself. Osmund knows the river better than anyone alive. He knows where it runs deep and where it lies, where the current pulls left under a calm skin, how it smells before rain and how it looks the morning before a flood. He reads it the way a scholar reads a book, all the way through and then again.

In the years when the ferry did not keep him busy, he fished as well, with a set of heavy nets he mended so many times that hardly a knot of the first ones is left. When his hands grew too stiff for the nets, he gave them to his daughter Agatha, and she mends them every evening on the step of the house, as he did. He still sits beside her while she does it, watching her fingers, and now and then he reaches over and pulls one knot tighter. That is the whole of his advice, and she takes it.

His wife Edda died twenty-two years ago, when Agatha was fifteen. After that it was the two of them on the water every day, and Osmund, who had never needed words much, found he needed even fewer with a daughter who could read his face. Agatha does the talking for both of them now, and she married Silas, who talks for everyone. Osmund approves of him with a nod he does not give to many.

### The family

His grandson Robin is nine, a mudlark, and the only person who claims to understand every one of Osmund's nods. Osmund lets him ride the ferry for nothing and steer it in the shallows. Last spring Robin dug an old badge out of the mud below the ferry landing and brought it to his grandfather, and Osmund looked at it for a long time and nodded once. Robin says that meant "put it somewhere safe". Osmund has never said what else it meant, if anything.

Aldith the herb wife rubs mint and comfrey salve into his stiff hands, and he nods his thanks, which from Osmund is a speech. Lucan the bargemaster hails the ferry in a voice that carries to both banks every time he passes, and Osmund lifts one hand from the pole in answer, as he has for twenty years.

### After dark

Osmund no longer crosses after dark. He stopped last summer, and he has not said why, not to Agatha, not to Aldith, not to anyone.

This is why. One evening at the end of last summer, crossing late with the light going, he felt the ferry lift under him in the deep channel, a hand's breadth, slowly, and settle again, as if something very long had passed beneath it without touching. Then a wake crossed the river ahead of him, against the current, with nothing at the head of it. He has seen things in the deep water before and since: shadows where no shadow should be, the surface bulging and smoothing over in still weather. He does not know what any of it is. He is sure only that it was not there when he was young.

He has not told his family because he does not know what to tell them, and Osmund does not speak unless he knows. But he watched Agatha's nets come up torn this spring, and he watched her move her fishing into the shallows without being asked, and he nodded.

### What he wants and fears

Osmund wants to pole the ferry until the day he cannot lift the pole, and then to hand it on to someone who will listen to the river. What he fears is the deep channel, and the day Robin decides he is old enough to find out what is in it.

A quest might begin with Osmund, for the first time anyone can remember, asking someone he trusts to cross with him after dark, or with the ferry found one morning drifting in the middle of the river with its pole gone.
