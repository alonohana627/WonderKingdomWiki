Hollis is twenty-four, a gate guard of Selsior, and he has the best view in the city and knows it. He stands twelve hours a day at the great gate, where the Royal Road runs out east past the Greenwood toward Kaldemor, and he still likes looking out over it. Most guards stop seeing the road after their first season. Hollis has not. He watches the light move across the fields, the carts come over the rise, the weather walk in from the east, and he would tell you, if you stood still long enough, that it is never the same road twice.

### The stern start

"State your business," he tells every traveller who comes up to the gate, in a voice as stern as he can make it. Then he looks at them properly, sees a tired face and a dusty pack, and the sternness falls off him like a cloak. "Ah, a traveller. Welcome." He cannot help it. Brann, the Gate Sergeant, taught him to start every greeting stern, because a gate guard who smiles first is a gate guard who gets lied to, and Hollis took the lesson to heart. He just never learned to finish the same way. Brann grumbles about it. He has never told him to stop, and Hollis has noticed that travellers tell him things they would never tell the Sergeant, and that Brann always wants to hear them.

He grew up in a narrow house in the inner streets of the city, where the only view from his window was the wall of the house opposite, close enough to touch. Perhaps that is why he loves the gate. His parents, a bricklayer and his wife, moved out to the farming country past the Greenwood when he was nineteen, to a cottage with a garden, and they write to him twice a year to tell him how far they can see from their door. He joined the Royal Guard the same year, scraped through his trials, and was sent to the gate a year later. He has asked for no other posting since.

### Mind the step

There is a worn step under the arch of the great gate, hollowed by centuries of feet, and it catches every third traveller who is looking up at the walls instead of down at the road. Hollis warns them all. "Mind the step," he says, "and the Sergeant," and the travellers laugh, and Brann, if he is in earshot, does not. It is the closest thing the gate has to a motto.

His friends are the gate's regulars. Tilda drives the Selsior cab from the stand just outside, and on slow afternoons she leans against her cab and Hollis leans against the gate and they trade news of the road: who came in hurt, which carters are late, which towns the cab will not go to because the passenger has never walked there. Tilda has never driven into the wilds and says that rule has never seemed wiser, and Hollis has started to agree with her. Then there is Biscuit, the gate dog, who greets Hollis every morning before he greets anyone else. Half the gate guards claim to have named the dog. Hollis claims it loudest, and has a long story about it that changes a little every time.

### The road, lately

Hollis likes the view, but he is not a fool, and the view has been changing. There are fewer travellers on foot, more wagons riding close together with hired swords on the tailboards, and more people coming in limping. Some evenings, when Baldwin's lamps are lit by the gate, Biscuit stands at the edge of their light with his ears up, staring into the dark, and Hollis finds himself staring too.

### What he wants and fears

Hollis wants to walk the Royal Road himself one day, all the way to the end of what he can see and past it, just to know what is there. What he fears is that by the time he gets the chance, the road will not be safe to walk.

A quest might begin with Hollis asking a traveller what is just over the rise to the east, because something has been moving there at dusk, or with a traveller he greeted stern and welcomed warmly who turns out to have been lying.
