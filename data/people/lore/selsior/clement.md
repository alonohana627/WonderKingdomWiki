Clement is forty-one, the Royal Herald of Selsior, and on the palace steps he is magnificent. He stands at the top of the red carpet in his tabard, unrolls the proclamation, fills his chest, and his voice rolls across the plaza and silences the Crown Market three streets away. Then he rolls the paper up, walks back inside, sits down on the first bench he finds and shakes. He is nervous in private and magnificent in public, and he has given up trying to be either one all the way through.

### The market crier

He grew up in the inner streets, the son of a tallow seller, both his parents long dead, and at sixteen he found work as a crier in the Crown Market. He called the opening of the stalls each dawn, cried lost purses and found dogs, and, more than anything, cried lost children: their names, their ages, what they were wearing, where their mothers were waiting. He was very good at it. A crowd went quiet when he called, and the children came. One morning Gideon, the Royal Herald, was buying apples when Clement cried a lost boy back to his father in under a minute, and the old man walked over and said, "Ha! Who taught you to breathe like that?" Nobody had. Gideon did, from then on, every week, in the empty plaza at first light before the market opened, until Clement could make himself heard over a festival.

Sixteen years ago Gideon cried the death of the old king and then the crowning of Queen Aveline, and heard the Great Bell ring for the second time in his life, and that year he handed the post on. He gave Clement's name to the palace and the palace took it. Clement was twenty-five. His first proclamation was the date of a harvest fair. He rehearsed it to the pigeons on the roofs for three days and still nearly fainted.

### Standing in a giant's boots

He has been herald for sixteen years now, and he still feels he is standing in a giant's boots. Gideon cried the expedition to the World Tree, the finding of the Children of the Tree and Cavelor, a king's death and a queen's crowning. Clement has cried fairs, new roads, treaty days, the Guard's trials, and lately, more and more, notices from the Knights' Court: which roads the patrols ride, which hours travellers should keep, that royal couriers now ride with guards. He rehearses every one of them to the pigeons first, on the roofs below Gideon's perch, and Gideon hears him, and tells him he is far better than he believes in a voice that half the plaza hears.

Every week Clement climbs the long ladder to the old man's perch and pretends it is only a social call. He brings something to eat, sits on the windowsill and talks about the weather for a while. Then, as if by accident, a folded paper comes out of his sleeve. Gideon pretends not to notice that it is never only a social call. Clement pretends not to notice that the old man has been waiting at the window since noon.

### The plaza

Clement lives alone in two rooms above a saddler's shop off the plaza, with a window on the palace steps. He has never married; he says he talks for a living and would like to come home to silence, though the truth is nearer that he is too shy to have asked. Everyone on the plaza knows him and greets him, and he answers each one by name in his magnificent voice and then blushes.

People bring him requests, because they think the herald must decide who performs at the palace. He does not, quite, though his word is listened to. Lambert, the juggler, has asked him about juggling for the queen at the next festival, and Clement was kind about it and promised nothing, because he could not bear to promise something he might not be able to keep. He has noticed the young singer from the Gilded Stage walk past him on the plaza several times as if about to speak to him. He has not worked out what she wants, and he is too shy to ask.

### What he wants and fears

Clement wants one day to cry a proclamation that the whole plaza cheers, the way Gideon says the crowd cheered on the day the Bell rang. What he fears is being handed news he cannot make a crowd believe, as the old man was handed the news of the expedition, or, worse, news that he can.

A quest might begin with Clement needing a proclamation carried to a crowd in another town, or with a sealed paper handed to him for crying at dawn that he is not sure the palace really sent.
