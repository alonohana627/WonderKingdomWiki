Lambert is twenty-two, a street juggler, and he draws the biggest crowds in the plaza. He juggles balls, clubs, rings, apples, other people's hats, and once, memorably, three of Piers's pies, of which he ate the third without breaking the pattern. He is showy, cheeky and kind to children, and he never drops a ball unless it makes the crowd laugh. When he does drop one, it rolls exactly to the feet of the smallest child watching, and he bows to them as if they had caught it.

### The puppeteer's apprentice

He was born in Selsior, in rooms over a shop at the bottom of Theatre Row, and he spent his childhood loitering at the puppet booth of Rafe, the puppeteer, who runs a show for the Row's children. Rafe is quiet, clever and more expressive with his hands than his face, and he speaks best through his puppets. One afternoon, when Lambert was about ten, Rafe noticed that the boy had stayed after the show again, and without a word he picked up three spare wooden puppet heads and began to juggle them. Then he handed them over. That was the first lesson. There were a great many more, nearly all of them silent, and by the time Lambert was fifteen he could keep five things in the air and make a crowd watch his face instead of his hands, which Rafe says is the real trick.

His parents, a shopkeeper and his wife, left the city for a quieter life in the farming country when Lambert was sixteen. He stayed. He had a pitch in the plaza by then and no intention of giving it up, and they write to him from time to time to ask whether he has found a proper job yet.

### The plaza

His pitch is on the plaza steps, beside the flower stall of Emmeline, who sells posies and makes the garlands for every festival in the square. They have been courting for a year. She can sell a posy to a man on his way to buy bread, and Lambert, who has watched her do it a hundred times, swears she could sell one to a juggler with his hands full; she proved it once by tucking a flower behind his ear mid-cascade. When his crowd disperses, they wander over to her stall, and when her customers wait, they watch him. They are, as Piers likes to tell everyone, very good for each other's business.

The children of the plaza adore him, none more than Mab, the pie seller's daughter, who copies every trick she sees and practises cartwheels at the foot of the Great Bell. Lambert has taught her to juggle two balls and is working on three, and he lets her pass the hat after his best shows. She keeps an honest count. Hugh, the Warden of the Great Bell, disapproves of all of it on principle and has been seen, more than once, counting the catches under his breath.

### For the queen

Lambert wants to juggle for the queen at the next royal festival. Not in the crowd, where any juggler might stand, but properly, invited, on the palace steps, with the court watching. He has worked out the routine to the last catch: seven rings, then the clubs, then a finish with a crown, borrowed from the Gilded Stage's prop room and very much not real. The trouble is that nobody seems to know how a street performer gets invited. He has asked Clement the herald, who was kind about it and promised nothing. He has thought about asking Gideon, the old herald up the ladder, who must have seen a hundred festivals.

### What he wants and fears

Lambert wants that invitation, and after it a shop of his own with Emmeline's flowers in the window. What he fears is the night a crowd stops laughing, because he has never found out what he would do then.

A quest might begin with Lambert needing someone to carry a request to the palace, or with the prop crown he borrowed for his routine turning out to be a good deal more valuable than the Gilded Stage believed.
