Gytha is thirty-five and runs the washing yard in a courtyard of the old quarters, where the lines are strung so thick between the walls that on a fine day you walk through the yard under a ceiling of other people's sheets. She has scrubbed the linen of half the lanes around her, and she can tell a household's whole week from its washing: who has been ill, who has been courting, who has been at the tavern, who has a guest they would rather the neighbours did not hear about. She does not repeat most of it. She does not need to. Knowing is enough, and everyone in the old quarters knows that she knows, which keeps a great many of them honest.

### A yard full of voices

She grew up in the lane where her father Simkin still mends shoes, the only child of Simkin and Winifred, and she learned early that the way to hold your own in a house with a storyteller and a grumbler in it is to talk faster than both of them. She is tough, sharp-tongued and quick to laugh, and the washing yard suits her: it is loud, hot, wet and full of women who talk while they work. Gytha talks loudest. She can scrub a stain out of a collar and a rumour out of a reputation with about the same effort, and she has done both for neighbours who needed it.

Her days start before light, with water hauled and fires lit under the coppers, and end after dark, when the last baskets go back to their houses. In between she argues prices, settles quarrels over whose shirt is whose and keeps the coppers from boiling dry.

### Hodge

Her son Hodge is ten. Hodge's father is gone, and Gytha does not talk about him. Not to Hodge, not to her neighbours, not even to her mother, who has a story for everything and has never once tried to tell that one. If someone in the yard is foolish enough to ask, Gytha changes the subject so briskly that they do not try twice. Whatever happened, she has decided it is hers to carry, and she carries it the way she carries a full basket, without complaint and without putting it down.

She is raising Hodge with her parents' help. Simkin gives him scraps of leather and lectures on the proper care of heels; Winifred gives him stories every evening on her doorstep. Gytha gives him rules, a great many of them, and the rule she means most is the one about the Royal Guard. Hodge wants to be a guard. Gytha has forbidden it, flatly and more than once. She wants him to learn a trade: something with tools in it, something that keeps him inside the city walls and brings him home every night. Her father's trade would do. So would any other. What will not do is a sword.

She has heard plenty about Hodge on the rooftops. Half the old quarters have told her, usually while handing over their washing. She has never once caught him at it, and she has her suspicions about how he gets home so fast. What she does not know, for all that she knows everyone else's secrets, is what he does up there at dawn. It has never crossed her mind to wonder what became of the old broom handle that used to lean in the corner of her own yard.

### What she wants and fears

Gytha wants Hodge safe, fed and apprenticed, in that order. She fears the roads, which have taken more people out of the gate than they have brought back lately, and she fears the day her son is old enough to walk out of it without asking her. Underneath that is a smaller fear that she keeps even quieter: that Hodge will one day ask the question she has never answered.

### Hooks

A quest might begin with Gytha finding a stain on someone's linen that she cannot place and does not like, or with her son finally being caught, and Gytha needing someone to help her decide what to do about it.
