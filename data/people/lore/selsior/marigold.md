Marigold is ten years old, a child actor of the Gilded Stage, and she has never in her life made an ordinary entrance. She does not come into a room; she arrives, the way Lavinia does, with a pause in the doorway to let the room notice her. She is precocious, dramatic and entirely sure of herself, and she knows every line of every play in the house, including the ones she is not in, better than the actors who are supposed to say them.

### Born in the second act

She is the daughter of Crispin, the house playwright, and Rosamund, the most famous actress in Selsior, and she has been on stage for as long as she can remember, which is longer than she can actually remember. As a baby she was carried on in a basket in the second act of The Baker's Wager. Edith the costumer sewed the lining of that basket, and still keeps a scrap of it in her attic; Marigold has asked for it twice and been refused twice, which she considers an outrage and also a very good scene. Since she could walk she has refused to leave the stage at all. She has played lost children, pages, a talking cat and the youngest of three princesses, and she has upstaged grown actors in every one.

She grew up in the wings, as her father did. She learned to read from prompt books, to count from the rows of the gallery, and to tell time by the interval bell. Tibb, who works the ropes and trapdoors, has taught her which boards creak and which ones drop, and she has sworn on her life never to use the trapdoor without asking. She has kept that oath for nearly a year, which is the longest she has kept any.

### Everyone's lines

Marigold prompts from the wings whether anyone asks her to or not. She can do Percival's heroic speeches with his exact pause before the last word, and she does them for the stagehands, who pay her in sweets. She can do her mother's faint and her mother's laugh. Rosamund, who recognises the type because she was the type, watches her daughter with pride and something close to alarm. Crispin finds her useful, which is worse: she remembers lines he cut three drafts ago and asks him why.

She has seen Mab, the pie seller's daughter, turning handsprings by the Great Bell, and she has made a point of saying, loudly, that the plaza is not a real stage. She has also practised a handspring in the dressing room, alone, and failed, and told no one.

### The part

Lavinia has bet everything on Crispin's new play about the Balrog War, and Marigold wants a part in it. Any part. There is no part for a child in it yet, and she means to fix that. She has suggested a refugee girl in the first act, a messenger at the truce, a drummer at the Last Stand, and a ghost, and her father has said "we'll see" so many times that she has stopped believing it.

So she has moved to stronger methods. Marigold knows where Crispin keeps his discarded endings. She has let him know that she knows, with a look over the breakfast table that she copied from her mother's best villainess. She has not said what she would do with them, which is, she has learned from the theatre, far more frightening than saying. Her father knows perfectly well that one of those endings is the one Lavinia would hate most, and that Marigold could find her way to Lavinia's office in her sleep.

She has not actually decided whether she would do it. She suspects she would not. But nobody else needs to know that.

### What she wants and fears

Marigold wants her name on a playbill, even small, and she wants the play to be the greatest thing Theatre Row has ever seen, because she would like to have been in it. Underneath the drama she is a child who has heard the grown-ups talking in low voices about the theatre's savings and the loan on the building, and what she fears is the night the Gilded Stage goes dark and there is nowhere left to make an entrance.

A quest might begin with one of Crispin's discarded endings going missing, and Marigold being the first suspect and, for once, innocent. Or it might begin with Marigold needing someone to fetch a prop from somewhere she has been forbidden to go, so that she can prove she deserves a part.
