"No time to talk," says Perrin, already past you. "Letters wait for no one." He is twenty-one, the Royal Courier, and he is always in a hurry, even when he is standing still on the plaza with his satchel on his hip, rocking on his heels as if the road might leave without him. He is fast-talking, self-important and very sure of his place in the order of things, which is, in his opinion, somewhere just below the queen and well above anyone who is in his way. "Out of my way," he calls across the plaza. "Official business." Usually it is.

### Letters for every town

Perrin carries the crown's letters to every town in the kingdom: up into the dry highlands of Midbarium, out to both harbours, down the Royal Road to Kaldemor, to Urbanograd and its arguments, and even to the World Tree. He will tell anyone who asks, and most who do not, which towns he has letters for today, and he always ends with the one for the World Tree, because it sounds best. He does not read the letters. He would like that to be clearly understood. He knows the seals, though, and he has carried some he is very proud to have carried, and he walks a little taller on the days the queen's own seal is in his satchel.

He calls himself the fastest courier in the kingdom, and he has the blisters to prove it, from saddle and boot alike, since the highland trails are walked as often as ridden; he will show you the blisters if you are not quick. He grew up in the city's inner streets, the son of a wheelwright and a laundress who have since moved to a village down the river and write to ask when he is going to visit. He ran messages for anyone who would pay him from the age of eight, and at seventeen, four years ago, he talked his way into the royal couriers by delivering a letter to the palace that nobody had asked him to carry, faster than the courier who had. He has been insufferable about it ever since.

### Tilda

His cousin Tilda, who drives the Selsior cab from the gate stand, is a good deal older than he is and a good deal less impressed. She tells everyone, fondly, that she can read from the mud on his boots exactly which roads he took, and that he takes the long way round far more often than he admits. He has asked her to stop. She has not. The worst of it is that she is right.

### The long way

The roads are not what they were, and Perrin knows it better than almost anyone, because he has ridden all of them. Stretches he used to gallop he now goes round. He knows where the wolves have moved, which bridge the boars have taken to sleeping under, and which bend of which highland trail has a thing on it he does not describe. Lately the royal couriers ride out with guards, by order of the Knights' Court, and Perrin grumbles about it at length: the guards are slow, the guards stop for lunch, the guards want to camp before dark. He grumbles less than he did. On one ride, which he does not talk about, he was glad of them.

Peterkin, the palace page, wants to be a courier when he grows up, and watches Perrin mount up in the plaza as if he were a hero in a play. Perrin pretends not to notice. He has started telling Peterkin, in a voice of great importance, which way the wind is blowing, which is the first thing he ever learned on the roads.

### What he wants and fears

Perrin wants to be the courier who gets through when no one else can, and to be thanked for it, loudly, by name. What he fears is the letter he cannot deliver, and having to come back to the plaza with it still sealed in his satchel.

Perrin also teaches **Courier's Stride**, the short burst of running speed every royal courier learns in their first week, to anyone who can keep pace with him from the palace steps to the gate and back without dropping a letter.

A quest might begin with Perrin, for once, asking someone to carry a letter for him on a road he will not take, or with a letter in his satchel that he is certain he never picked up.
