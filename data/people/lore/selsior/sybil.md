Sybil is sixty-one, the Royal Wardrobe Mistress, and she can judge a person by their hem. A hem let down tells her someone has grown; a hem taken up in a hurry tells her someone is going somewhere they should not; a hem with mud on the inside tells her rather more than its owner would like. She is elegant, sharp-tongued and kind underneath, and she keeps the royal family's clothes, from the coronation robes in their cedar chest to the princess's riding boots, with the precision of a general and the patience of a saint who has stopped expecting thanks.

### Two sisters, two halves

She and her elder sister Edith grew up in the city, in a house where both girls were sewing before they could spell; their parents are dead now. Edith, five years older, went to Theatre Row at eighteen and has dressed its players ever since. Sybil went the other way. She came to the palace at nineteen, in the old king's reign, as a seamstress in the wardrobe rooms, and she has been Wardrobe Mistress for twenty-five years. She altered the coronation robes for Queen Aveline sixteen years ago, working through the night with pins in her mouth and her heart in her throat, and she still says it was the finest fit she ever made.

She never married. She says she has dressed enough brides to know what it costs.

The sisters meet for supper once a week and trade gossip about who wore what: which envoy's wife wore last year's sleeves, which actor split his breeches in the third act, what the queen chose for the trade council and why. Edith says they dress the two halves of Selsior, the half that is real and the half that pretends. Sybil says that on a good week she cannot tell them apart either.

### A palace that talks

The palace is full of people who would like to know things. Agnes, the maid of the upper halls, hears everything and repeats half of it, and Sybil is the one person in the household from whom Agnes has never got a single story. Sybil regards this as a professional achievement. She is sharp with the maids, sharper with the pages and sharpest of all with anyone who touches the coronation robes without washing their hands, and the whole household knows that under the edge she will mend a torn sleeve before a scolding is noticed, and say nothing about how it was torn.

### The cloak

Last autumn, Princess Elinor came to the wardrobe rooms early one morning and asked for a cloak: plain, grey, with a deep hood, nothing anyone would look at twice. Sybil looked at her, at her hands and at her boots, and fetched one from the back of a press without a word. She knew where the princess meant to wear it. She has known ever since, and she has made it her business to help. It was Sybil who suggested that "riding" was a perfectly good reason for a princess to be out early, and Sybil who keeps the riding boots polished and the riding habit worn just enough to be believed. She mends the cloak when it comes back torn. When anyone asks where that grey cloak went, she says she has no idea, in a voice that closes the subject.

She is not sure what she would say if the queen asked. She has noticed Aveline looking at Elinor's hands at breakfast, and she has noticed that Aveline has not asked anyone anything yet. Edith has noticed that there is one subject her sister goes quiet on. Sybil has noticed Edith noticing, and has served her more soup.

### What she wants and fears

Sybil wants to dress the princess for one more great occasion, and she hopes privately that it will be one Elinor has earned. What she fears is the day the cloak comes back with blood on it that is not from a blister.

A quest might begin with Sybil needing a rare thread or cloth for a royal garment that must be ready by a date she will not explain, or with a garment going missing from the wardrobe rooms that Sybil did not lend to anyone.
