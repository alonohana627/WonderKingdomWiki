Dickon is forty-one, the skywalk carpenter of the old quarters, and he does his best work hanging over thin air. He builds and mends the narrow wooden walkways that cross above the old quarters' streets from one upper storey to another, and he does it with a rope round his waist, a mallet in his belt, a mouthful of nails and a whistle that carries three lanes. He is fearless, cheerful and a little reckless. The people underneath his walks have learned to look up when the whistling stops, because that is when something is about to fall, and it is usually a nail and occasionally Dickon.

### Born under the walks

He grew up in the old quarters, in a garret room under a roof that had a skywalk running past its window, and as a small boy he spent more time on that walk than in the room. At thirteen he was apprenticed to old Godric, the skywalk carpenter of those days, who was then past sixty and no longer liked heights, and who stood in the street shouting instructions while Dickon did the climbing. Godric taught him which timbers bend before they break and which break without warning, how to hang a walk so it sways without twisting, and how to talk a frightened householder into letting a stranger nail a plank to their window. When Godric died, Dickon took over his tools and his walks, and he has kept them ever since.

There are more than forty skywalks in the old quarters now, a second city of planks and rails above the winding lanes. Dickon has built a dozen of them himself and mends all the rest. He knows each one by its creak.

### Ida

His wife is Ida, the night watchwoman of the old quarters. They have been married twelve years and have no children. He works by day and she works by night, so they meet twice a day, at dawn when she comes home and at dusk when she goes out, and they have made those two meetings into something better than most people's whole evenings. He married her, he says, because she can tell from the sound of a plank whether it will hold; she says she married him because he is the only man in Selsior who has never once asked her whether she is frightened on her rounds. He never will. He knows better than anyone how frightening the dark under the old quarters can be, because she tells him, at dawn, about the scratching under the drains that keeps pace with her and stops when she stops. He listens, and makes her tea, and has lately started building his walks with an extra brace, though he would deny it was for that reason.

Edwina the midwife came to their wedding uninvited, as she comes to everyone's, because she delivered Ida and has the name in her book. Dickon was delighted and gave her the best seat.

### The long walk

Dickon has a dream, and it hangs from the kitchen ceiling in the shape of a model made of matchwood and string. He wants to build a skywalk long enough to cross from the old quarters to Theatre Row without anyone touching the ground: one great span over the broad street at the foot of the Row, half as long again as any walk in the city, landing on the roof of the Gilded Stage itself. He has worked out the timbers and the bracing and most of the cost. He has climbed the theatre's roof with a measuring line twice, the second time with Tibb the stagehand holding the other end and asking pointed questions. He has not yet asked Lavinia, who owns the roof, and he suspects she will either throw him out or want her name painted along the whole length of it.

Lately he has had a smaller puzzle. On his rounds he has twice found small fresh footprints in the pitch on the newest walks, at dawn, before anyone should have been up there. Somebody young knows the roofs very well. Dickon has not told anyone. He would quite like to meet them.

### What he wants and fears

Dickon wants the long walk built in his lifetime and Ida beside him on the day it opens, walking above the city in daylight for once. What he fears is not falling. It is the morning Ida does not come home at dawn.

A quest might begin with Dickon needing timber long and sound enough for the great span, from a forest the carters no longer like to visit, or with a skywalk found cut through in the night, from below.
