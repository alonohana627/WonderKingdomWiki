Wulfric is thirty-eight, the Warden of the Royal Vault, and he is the largest man most people in the palace have ever seen. He has to duck under nearly every door below stairs and turn sideways on the narrow ones, and when he stands before the vault's great iron-banded doors in his grey mail, with his hands folded on the pommel of his sword, visitors tend to lower their voices without knowing why. Then he speaks, and it is the softest voice in the palace, and they have to lean in to hear it. He is huge, quiet and gentle, and if you thank him for anything, even for holding a door, he turns pink to the ears.

### From the quarries to the vault

He grew up upstream, in a quarrymen's village where the stone for half of Selsior is cut, the son of a quarryman who could lift a block two men could not, and of a mother who sang while she split slate. Both of them are dead now. Wulfric was already the size of a doorway at fifteen, and the quarry would have taken him gladly, but he wanted to see the capital, and at eighteen he walked down the river and joined the Royal Guard.

The Guard put him on the palace gates, where he stood for eight years, and where it was discovered that nobody, not even the drunkest reveller on a festival night, ever argued with him. Twelve years ago, when the old warden of the vault retired, Aldous, the Keeper of the Royal Vault, came up to the gate to look at him, counted him twice, as he counts everything, and smiled once. Wulfric has guarded the vault doors ever since. In all that time he has never once had to draw his sword there. He oils it every week anyway, and polishes the scabbard, and says the sword is a kind of promise and a promise ought to be kept bright.

### Jocelyn

The vault's clerk, Jocelyn, had been at her desk for three years when Wulfric arrived. He noticed her on his first day. It took him years to do anything about it. What he did, at first, was carry her ledgers. Whenever the great bound books had to go up the stairs to the palace, Wulfric was somehow there, and he would pick up the whole stack without a word and carry it up behind her, and carry it down again after. He did this for years. It took Jocelyn a surprisingly long time to notice that he was never anywhere else when the ledgers needed carrying.

When she did notice, she asked him straight out whether he was going to say anything. He blushed to the ears and said he had been working up to it. They married four years ago. He still carries the ledgers. She labels his lunch, and the hook he hangs his cloak on, and he keeps every one of the labels in a tin, though he has not told her that.

### Below the palace

The vault is quiet, cool and very old, and Wulfric loves it. He knows its corridors by the sound of his own boots, and he can tell from the echo when a door has been left ajar three rooms away. He knows that Jocelyn has found a locked box in the oldest records room that is in none of the ledgers, and that it keeps her awake. He cannot open it for her and would not if he could. But he has started, on his rounds, walking past the oldest records room twice instead of once.

### What he wants and fears

Wulfric wants a quiet watch, a full ledger and his wife's hand on his arm on the stairs. What he fears is the day he has to draw his sword below the palace, and the thing that would make him.

A quest might begin with Wulfric hearing, on a still night, an echo in the vault's deepest corridor that matches no door he knows, or with him asking a traveller, with great embarrassment, to help him choose a present for Jocelyn that she will not immediately label.
