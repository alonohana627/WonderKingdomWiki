Bartholomew is seventy-seven, the Master of the Royal Academy, and if you ask him a question he will answer it with a better one. He is grandfatherly, rambling and sharp as a tack beneath it. He will begin to tell you about the weather, wander through a story about a goat he knew as a boy and a disagreement he had with a stargazer in his forties, and arrive, quite suddenly, at the exact thing you were trying not to think about.

### A shepherd's boy among the Magicians

He did not grow up among books. He was a shepherd's son from the open downs north of the capital, and he came to Selsior at fourteen with a letter from a village schoolteacher and no idea what an academy was. The Royal Academy was founded by the city's first Magicians, and in those days it still felt to a farm boy like a house full of old wizards who all knew something he did not. He decided, within a week, that the way to catch up was to ask them all questions. He has never stopped.

He studied there for ten years and began teaching at twenty-four. He was a teacher for most of his life before the other masters made him Master of the Academy, twenty-one years ago, and he still teaches a class every season, because he says a Master who does not teach is just a man with a large office. His office is indeed large, and it is buried under papers, and somewhere in it is a chair for visitors that nobody has found in years.

### The Academy's pride

He welcomes students from every town on Veligor and is fiercely proud that no two of them think alike. He has had highland students who argue with their fists, forest students who argue in riddles, harbour students who argue with the tide tables and Urbanograd students who argue with everything. He wants all of them. He says the Academy was made by people who once fought a war over whose way of thinking was right, and that the least it can do now is to keep every way of thinking in one building and make them eat lunch together.

He likes nothing better than a student who answers back. When Mariota's father died and the bindery went to pay his debts, Bartholomew made sure she kept the place she had won with an essay that argued with its own examiners. He has taken a long, patient interest in Thomasin, the historian, who is his protegee and who hates a good story that is not true, and he has watched with pleasure as she became the person the Academy sends for when someone wants to know what really happened in the Balrog War. He has also watched, with less pleasure, how often she is being asked.

### Morwen

His oldest friend at the Academy is Morwen, who teaches its young mages. He has known her since she came to teach there twenty-eight years ago, a young teacher with very firm opinions, and the two of them have argued over supper about once a week ever since. She says he answers her questions with better questions and that it is infuriating. He says it is the only way to stop her answering them herself before she has finished asking. They would each defend the other to the death, and both of them know it.

### What he wants and fears

Bartholomew wants the Academy to keep asking questions, even now, when the kingdom wants answers. He knows the roads are worse every season. He knows Morwen's work on the old seal-craft is no longer only history, and that Theobald's star charts and Mariota's reading of them are looking for something they have not yet named. What he fears is the day someone from the palace comes to the Academy and asks it for an answer it does not have, and his students, who have never once agreed with each other, all agree to go and find it.

A quest might begin with Bartholomew asking someone to deliver a question, not a letter, to a scholar in another town, or with a student who has stopped coming to class and left behind a notebook the Master would very much like to read.
