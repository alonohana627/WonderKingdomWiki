Gideon is seventy-eight, the retired Royal Herald of Selsior, and he still has the loudest voice in the city. He does not use it as often as he would like. These days he lives in a perch high above the plaza, a little room under the eaves at the top of a long ladder, and when someone does climb up to see him, he greets them with a great booming "Ha!" that sends the pigeons off the sill. He is booming, nostalgic and a little lonely, and he laughs loudest at his own stories, which is just as well, because he tells a great many of them.

### Forty years on the steps

Gideon cried King Bertram's news in the plaza for forty years. He was twenty and standing in the crowd on the day Bertram was crowned, fifty-eight years ago, when the Great Bell rang over the square, and he has said ever since that the sound went straight into his chest and stayed there. Two years later the herald's post fell vacant, and Gideon, who had been practising proclamations to the river for most of his youth, walked up the palace steps and asked for it. He got it, he says, because nobody else in the city could make himself heard over the Crown Market on a busy morning.

From then on, every piece of royal news in Selsior came out of Gideon's mouth first: new laws and new roads, harvest fairs and treaty days, the births and deaths of great people. Fifty years ago, a young man of twenty-eight, he cried the setting out of the Great World Tree Expedition from the palace steps to the biggest crowd he had ever seen. Later he cried the news of what it found, the Children of the Tree and the guardians of Cavelor beneath the roots, and he says no news he ever gave was harder to make a crowd believe.

His last two proclamations came in the same year, sixteen years ago. He cried the death of the old king, and a little while later he cried the crowning of Queen Aveline, and he heard the Great Bell ring for the second time in his life. He handed the post on that year, and he has never stood on the steps in an official capacity since.

### The perch

He chose the perch because from up there he can hear every bell in Selsior: the clock tower bells that the palace children race, the drill bell at the Knights' Court, the market's opening bell, the bell on the gatehouse, and of course the Great Bell on its dais, which does not ring. He knows them all by voice, the way other old men know birds. If he has ever heard anything from the Great Bell in the long years since the coronation, he has not said so, and for a man who says everything, that is a little unusual.

The trouble with a perch is the ladder. Nobody climbs it to hear Gideon any more. Clement, who took over the herald's post, climbs up every week, and pretends it is only a social call, and then asks his advice on a proclamation. Gideon pretends not to notice that it is never only a social call. He has heard Clement rehearsing to the pigeons on the roofs below, and he thinks the young man is far better than he believes, and tells him so in a voice that half the plaza hears. Clement says he is standing in a giant's boots. Gideon, privately, remembers being exactly as nervous on those steps himself, and has never quite admitted it.

His oldest friend is Aldous, the Keeper of the Royal Vault, who counts twice and smiles once and does not climb ladders. So once a month Gideon climbs down instead, slowly, and walks to the palace to sit with Aldous when the vault closes. They have served the crown for most of their lives, and they talk about the old king's reign the way other people talk about a favourite play.

### What he wants and fears

Gideon wants a visitor. He has sixty years of stories about the plaza and the people in it, and he would like to tell them to someone who has not heard them, and he would like that someone to laugh in the right places, or at least after he does. What he fears is that the stories will go when he does, and that nobody will remember how the square sounded on the day the Bell rang.

A quest might begin with Gideon asking a traveller to carry a message down to Clement that he is too proud to deliver himself, or with the old herald hearing, from his perch, a bell he does not recognise.
