Osbert is sixty-nine, the Royal Steward, and he can tell how many candles burned in the great hall last night by sniffing the air in it this morning. He is usually right to within two. He runs the palace household: its kitchens and cellars, its linen and silver, its pages and maids, its fires, its accounts, its locks. He has done so for thirty-four years, longer than Queen Aveline has worn the crown, and there is no spoon in the palace and very few secrets whose whereabouts he does not know.

### Up from the boot room

He came to the palace at fourteen as a boot boy, in the early years of King Bertram's long reign, and he has never lived anywhere else since. He polished boots, then silver, then carried trays, then kept the cellar book, and he learned the household the way other people learn a language, from the bottom up. When old Steward Hamlyn died thirty-four years ago, the old king gave Osbert the great ring of keys, and Osbert has carried it ever since. The palace staff who served Bertram still call him "the old king", and Osbert does too, but it was Osbert who stood at the back of the plaza when the Great Bell rang sixteen years ago, and Osbert who saw to it that the new queen's household was ready for her before she had finished being crowned.

Aveline trusts him with keys she would give to no one else, and he knows it. He has never married. When anyone asks, he says the household is the only family that has ever needed him to count its spoons.

### The household

Osbert is precise and dry, and his praise is so rare that the staff treasure it. Cuthbert, the royal cook, shouts at the pots and sings to the bread, and Osbert has spent years trying to make him do neither in the presence of envoys. He has failed, and secretly he is glad. Agnes, the maid who cleans the upper halls, hears everything and repeats only half of it, which in Osbert's view is still half too much. Peterkin, the page, runs everywhere, and Osbert has told him a hundred times that a royal messenger walks, and has never once meant it.

He is secretly sentimental. In a drawer of his desk he keeps the menu from the coronation feast, a beetle in a jar that Alaric once gave him as a present, and a list in his own hand of every person who has served in the household in his time, with the date each one left.

### The keys and the secrets

The thing Osbert wants most is to retire. He would like a small room with a window and nobody to count anything for. But retiring means handing the keys to someone, and he has not yet found a person he trusts with all of them. He has trained under-stewards. They have been honest and hard-working and not quite enough.

Part of the trouble is that the keys open more than doors. Osbert knows things. He knows that someone has been drawing the bolt of the little door in the kitchen garden wall before dawn, most mornings, and sliding it back by breakfast. He knows whose boots come back muddy. He has said nothing to anyone, telling himself that it is not a lie if nobody asks him, and that he is not quite sure. He is quite sure. He also knows what is, or is not, behind the tapestries in the throne hall, and when Prince Alaric asks him, he raises an eyebrow and says nothing at all, which he knows is the most tormenting answer available.

### What he fears

Osbert fears the day he is asked a direct question about that garden door. He fears, too, being the steward who handed the keys to the wrong person, and so he keeps carrying them, and his knees get older.

A quest might begin with a key missing from Osbert's ring for the first time in thirty-four years, or with the old steward quietly testing someone to see whether they could be trusted with it.
