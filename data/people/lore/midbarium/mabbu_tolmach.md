Mabbu is seventy-seven, and she has walked every herding path in the highlands at least a hundred times. She is sharp-tongued, sharp-eyed and endlessly hospitable, and she will scold a guest and feed them in the same breath. Travelers who come to the Tolmach home camp hungry leave full, slightly bruised in their dignity, and usually with a better idea of where to find water on the road home than they had when they arrived.

### The walking years

Mabbu was born in a hide tent on the high pastures in a hard, dry year, and she likes to say that she learned to walk so she could follow the goats to the next spring. The Tolmach have always lived by knowing where water is, and Mabbu learned it better than anyone of her generation. As a girl she lived through the driest year anyone in the camps can remember, when spring after spring failed along the south path, and she has never forgotten which ones kept running. The Greywater spring, in its cleft halfway down that path, was one of them. She has told that story so often that her grandchildren can recite it with her.

She married Garrak, a herder with a famous nose for weather, and they followed the herds together for over thirty years. Garrak died twenty years ago, of a long illness that kept him in the home camp for his last winter. Mabbu went back out with the herds the following spring, because, she said, the goats did not know he was gone and somebody had to explain it to them.

Their daughters are Sennu, now chieftain of the Tolmach, and Harra, who sells the herds' wool and cheese at the Trading Post. Mabbu raised Sennu on the paths, and Sennu has her patience. She raised Harra on the paths too, and Harra has her voice. Mabbu is proud of both and tells neither, except loudly to other people within their hearing.

### The home camp

About five years ago, Mabbu's knees gave out on a steep climb above the terraces, and she has not followed the herds since. She keeps the home camp now: the cluster of tents and stone shelters closest to town, where the Tolmach leave their elders, their youngest children and their stores. She runs it like a chieftain runs a family, and Sennu, who actually is one, has learned not to argue.

Mabbu remembers out loud. Every spring that ever ran in every dry year, every path that ever washed out, every herder who ever got lost and how they were found. Sennu listens, knowing that one day those memories will be the only map left. Her grandson Kesh, Harra's boy, asks her to tell it all again, which delights her. Her other grandson, Ennek, Sennu's son, became a canyon guide on her stories, and she considers every trader he brings up safely to be partly her doing.

Lessa, the Brenna bonesetter, walks out to the home camp every few days with salve for Mabbu's knees. Mabbu grumbles through every visit, tells her she is doing it wrong, feeds her far too much and asks after her daughter Neffa's singing. She would be deeply offended if Lessa ever stopped coming.

### What she fears

This year Sennu has kept the herds closer to town than ever before, and the home camp is crowded with animals that ought to be on the high pastures. Mabbu can see the grass thinning from her own tent. She says nothing to Sennu, who has enough to carry, but she has started walking a little farther each morning on her bad knees, testing them, in case the family needs her on the paths again.

She does not yet know that the Greywater spring has gone dry. When she learns it, she will know at once that it never failed in the worst year of her life, and she will want to know why it has failed now.

### Hooks

A quest could carry Mabbu's memories of the old springs to a place no one has checked in years, or help her up a path one last time to see a pasture she has not seen since her knees gave out.
